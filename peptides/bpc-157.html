BPC-157 side effects and safety: what human studies show

Evidence reviewed: November 15, 2026. Updated November 20, 2026.

BPC-157 is not FDA-approved, and its side effects in people are largely unknown. The FDA says it lacks sufficient information to know whether compounded BPC-157 would cause harm in humans. It is not approved for human use, and it is banned in sport. This page is not medical advice. [FDA] [USADA]

No products are linked or recommended on this page.

TL;DR

  • There is no established human safety profile for BPC-157. Only three small human reports exist, with 12, 12 and 2 participants, and none had a control group (Grade C). [PMIDs 34324435, 39325560, 40131143]
  • Two of those reports said no side effects occurred; the third did not report on side effects in its abstract. Reports this small cannot rule out common, rare or long-term harms. [PMIDs 39325560, 40131143, 34324435]
  • A 2025 systematic review of 36 studies (35 animal or laboratory, 1 clinical) found no clinical safety data. [PMID 40756949]
  • The FDA flags possible immune reactions (immunogenicity) for some routes and problems with peptide impurities. [FDA]
  • Athletes: BPC-157 is prohibited at all times under the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) list of non-approved substances. [USADA] [WADA S0 text]
  • Reviews warn that gray-market products raise concerns about identity, purity, concentration and sterility (Grade C). [PMIDs 42937313, 40756949]
  • BPC-157 vs TB-500: neither is FDA-approved, and the only direct comparison found was a rat study. [PMIDs 42542926, 42937313]

What side effects of BPC-157 have been reported in people?

Very few, mostly because very few people have been studied. Here is everything the published human reports say about side effects:

Human reportPeopleHow it was givenSide effects reported
Knee pain chart review, one Florida clinic [PMID 34324435]16 reached by phone; 12 had BPC-157 aloneInjection into the kneeThe abstract does not report on side effects
Interstitial cystitis pilot [PMID 39325560]12 womenInjections around the bladder during cystoscopy (10 mg total, single procedure)“No adverse events were reported”
Intravenous safety pilot [PMID 40131143]2 adults who had used IV BPC-157 beforeIV infusion on two days (10 mg, then 20 mg)No side effects reported; tested heart, liver, kidney, thyroid and glucose markers did not measurably change

All three share the same lead author, were small, and had no comparison group. Grade C. A later review counted the same three pilot studies and concluded that no adverse effects were reported but rigorous, large-scale trials are lacking. [PMID 40789979]

Why can’t these studies show that BPC-157 is safe?

“No side effects reported” in 2 or 12 people is not the same as “safe.” A side effect that affects 1 in 100 users would usually be missed in a group that small. The studies were also short or retrospective. The knee review relied on phone calls months after injections, and the intravenous pilot followed two people for three days. [PMIDs 34324435, 40131143]

The 2025 systematic review put it plainly: no clinical safety data were found. Animal safety studies showed no adverse effects across several organ systems, but animal results do not establish human safety. The review added that adverse effects are possible because of unregulated manufacturing, contamination, or unknown clinical safety. [PMID 40756949]

What about the BPC-157 side effects people report online?

Clinic pages, forums and videos list effects such as injection-site redness, headache, nausea, dizziness and tiredness. We found none of these recorded in the three published human reports: two said no adverse events occurred and the third did not report on side effects in its abstract. That does not mean these effects never happen. It means no study has measured them, so nobody can say how common they are or whether BPC-157 caused them. [PMIDs 39325560, 40131143, 34324435]

Can BPC-157 cause cancer?

No study has shown that BPC-157 causes cancer in people, and no human study has tested it. The concern is theoretical. In animal research, one proposed way BPC-157 works is by sending signals that grow new blood vessels, and a 2026 review lists abnormal blood-vessel growth among the theoretical risks of unapproved injectable peptides. Grade C. [PMIDs 40756949, 40789979, 42757290]

Is oral BPC-157 safer than injections?

Nobody knows. None of the three published human reports tested an oral form: they used injections into the knee, injections around the bladder, and intravenous infusion. No study compares routes in people. The HHS interim policy lists oral and topical forms alongside injections for licensed compounders, but that is a regulatory condition, not evidence that any form is safe. [PMIDs 34324435, 39325560, 40131143] [HHS policy document]

What safety risks does the FDA describe for BPC-157?

The FDA lists BPC-157 among bulk drug substances that may present significant safety risks in compounding (in the “nominated but withdrawn” table on a page last updated April 22, 2026). Its exact wording: [FDA]

Compounded drugs containing BPC-157 may pose risk for immunogenicity for certain routes of administration and may have complexities with regard to peptide-related impurities and active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) characterization. FDA has identified no, or only limited, safety-related information for the proposed routes of administration. Therefore, the agency lacks sufficient information to know whether the drug would cause harm when administered to humans.

Immunogenicity means the body may mount an immune reaction against the peptide. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review their safety, effectiveness or quality before they are sold. [FDA]

What are the risks of research-use or gray-market BPC-157?

A 2026 orthopaedic review says commercial peptide products marketed as BPC-157 or TB-500 raise concerns about identity, purity, concentration, sterility and manufacturing consistency. It found no validated dosing, pharmacokinetic or long-term safety data, and concluded that routine clinical use cannot currently be recommended. Grade C (narrative review). [PMID 42937313]

A second 2026 review groups BPC-157 with other unapproved injectables and lists potential risks for these products as a class, including contamination, manufacturing impurities and inaccurate dosing. It also raises theoretical concerns such as abnormal blood-vessel growth. These are concerns raised by reviewers, not side effects observed in BPC-157 studies. Grade C. [PMID 42757290]

Both orthopaedic reviews advise clinicians to talk with athletes about anti-doping and testing rules. [PMIDs 42937313, 40756949]

Are there BPC-157 side effects specific to women?

No sex-specific safety data were found. The only women-only study was the 12-person interstitial cystitis pilot, which reported no adverse events. That is far too few people to describe risks for women. None of the sources reviewed here report data on pregnancy or breastfeeding. Grade C. [PMID 39325560]

What is BPC-157, and what does the rest of the evidence show?

BPC-157, or Body Protection Compound-157, is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide related to a sequence described from gastric juice. Reviews describe it as investigational. [PMIDs 40756949, 40789979, 40005999]

Most of the evidence comes from animals. In animal injury models, BPC-157 improved muscle, tendon, ligament and bone outcomes, and proposed mechanisms include blood-vessel growth signals and lower inflammatory cytokines. Those findings cannot establish that it heals injuries or reduces inflammation in people. [PMIDs 40756949, 40789979]

The human knee review reported that 11 of 12 people who had BPC-157 alone described significant improvement, but there was no control group and no standard measurement tool. Grade C. [PMID 34324435] The interstitial cystitis pilot reported complete symptom resolution in 10 of 12 women; it was unblinded and uncontrolled. Grade C. [PMID 39325560]

BPC-157 vs TB-500: how do they compare?

They are different peptides, and they are often sold or discussed together. Neither has an FDA-approved use or controlled human trials for injuries. [PMID 42937313]

FDA approvalNot approvedNot approved

Full-length thymosin beta-4 has been studied in people for eye, skin and heart conditions, but a 2026 review notes that those findings cannot establish musculoskeletal benefit or safety, and that TB-500 has no validated biological or clinical equivalence to the full protein. [PMID 42937313]

The only head-to-head comparison found was in 32 rats with repaired Achilles tendons. TB-500 showed a significant strength advantage at four weeks, BPC-157 did not reach significance on total scores, and combining them added nothing. A rat study cannot tell anyone which peptide is safer or works better in people. Grade C. [PMID 42542926]

Is BPC-157 FDA-approved or allowed in compounding?

BPC-157 is not FDA-approved. On October 30, 2026, HHS published a draft, interim enforcement-discretion policy for 503A compounding with certain peptides. Its Appendix A lists six peptides, including BPC-157 (the others are Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax and TB-500). HHS says it does not intend to act against licensed pharmacies and doctors who compound them under set conditions, including a prescription for a specific patient, raw peptide from an FDA-registered manufacturer with a certificate of analysis, and reporting serious side effects and quality problems to the FDA. For BPC-157, Appendix B limits compounded products to injection under the skin or into muscle, oral and topical forms. [HHS policy document]

The Federal Register notice says the policy “does not establish any rights for any person and is not binding on HHS or the public,” and HHS says it may revisit the policy if a safety signal or quality problem appears. It is not FDA approval and not an addition to the 503A bulks list, and it covers licensed compounders only, not peptides sold online as “research use” products. [HHS Federal Register notice]

Is BPC-157 banned in sport?

Yes. The US Anti-Doping Agency says BPC-157 is prohibited under the S0 Non-Approved Substances category of the WADA Prohibited List, and that it is not approved for human clinical use by the FDA. [USADA] WADA’s S0 wording names BPC-157 as an example and says substances in this class, which no government health regulator has approved for human therapeutic use, are prohibited at all times, not only in competition. [WADA S0 text]

How should I read the evidence grades on this page?

  • Grade A: a published Phase 3 randomized trial, or a systematic review of randomized trials.
  • Grade B: Phase 2 trials, exploratory analyses, small controlled human studies, or cohorts.
  • Grade C: uncontrolled or retrospective reports, animal or laboratory research, and narrative reviews.

Every human BPC-157 report reviewed here is Grade C. A systematic review made up mostly of animal studies does not raise human claims to Grade A.

Frequently asked questions

What are the side effects of BPC-157?

Nobody knows the side-effect profile of BPC-157 in people. Two small uncontrolled human reports (12 and 2 people) reported no side effects and a third (12 people) did not report on them, but studies that small cannot detect common, rare or long-term harms. A 2025 systematic review found no clinical safety data. [PMIDs 39325560, 40131143, 40756949]

Is BPC-157 safe?

Safety has not been established. The FDA says it lacks sufficient information to know whether compounded BPC-157 would cause harm in humans, and flags possible immunogenicity and peptide-impurity problems. Reviews also warn about unregulated manufacturing and contamination. [FDA] [PMIDs 40756949, 42937313]

Are there BPC-157 side effects specific to women?

No sex-specific safety data were found. The only study limited to women was a pilot of 12 women with interstitial cystitis that reported no adverse events, which is far too small to define risks for women. None of the sources reviewed report data on pregnancy or breastfeeding. [PMID 39325560]

How does BPC-157 compare with TB-500?

Neither is FDA-approved and neither has controlled human trials for injuries. TB-500 is a seven-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4 and is often confused with the full protein. The FDA says it has not identified any human exposure data for TB-500. The only direct comparison found was a 32-rat tendon study. [PMIDs 42937313, 42542926] [FDA]

Is BPC-157 FDA-approved?

No. BPC-157 is not an FDA-approved drug. A draft HHS enforcement-discretion policy for some compounded peptides is not FDA approval and is not the same as adding a substance to the 503A bulks list. [FDA]

Does BPC-157 help injuries or inflammation in people?

That is not established. Most evidence is animal or laboratory research, and the human reports are small and uncontrolled. Animal findings cannot prove injury healing or anti-inflammatory benefit in people. [PMIDs 40756949, 40789979, 42937313]

Is BPC-157 legal?

BPC-157 is not FDA-approved for any use. Under a draft, interim HHS policy published October 30, 2026, licensed pharmacies and doctors may compound it for a specific patient under set conditions, but the policy is not binding and does not cover “research use” products sold online. It is also prohibited for athletes under WADA rules. This covers the US federal position only and is not legal advice. [FDA] [HHS policy document] [USADA]

Is BPC-157 banned by WADA?

Yes. USADA says BPC-157 is prohibited under the S0 Non-Approved Substances category of the WADA Prohibited List, and WADA’s S0 wording names BPC-157 and says this class is prohibited at all times. [USADA] [WADA S0 text]

Can BPC-157 cause cancer?

That has not been shown, and no human study has tested it. Reviewers raise a theoretical concern because animal research suggests BPC-157 may promote new blood-vessel growth, and one 2026 review lists abnormal blood-vessel growth among possible risks of unapproved injectable peptides. [PMIDs 40756949, 42757290]

Is oral BPC-157 safer than injections?

Nobody knows. None of the three published human reports tested an oral form, and no study compares routes in people. [PMIDs 34324435, 39325560, 40131143]
