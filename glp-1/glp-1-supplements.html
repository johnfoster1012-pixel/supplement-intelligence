Do GLP-1 supplements, drops and boosters work? What the evidence shows

Short answer. No supplement sold as a "GLP-1 booster" or "natural GLP-1" has been shown to work like a GLP-1 drug. In its main trial, people on semaglutide lost about 15% of their body weight, while people on a placebo lost about 2%. The best-studied supplement ingredients led to only about 0 to 2 kg (0 to 4 lb) more weight loss than a placebo in pooled trials, depending on the ingredient and the analysis, and several showed no clear effect.

Some ingredients do raise the body's own GLP-1 a little or modestly improve blood sugar. That is not the same thing: natural GLP-1 is broken down within minutes, while semaglutide is built to last about a week. We found no published trial of any GLP-1 patch, drop or gummy product.

What "GLP-1 supplement" means

GLP-1 is a gut hormone released after eating. It signals fullness and helps control blood sugar. Drugs such as semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) copy and outlast it.

A "GLP-1 supplement" contains no GLP-1 drug. The label usually means one of three things:

  • an ingredient claimed to raise your own GLP-1, such as a citrus flavonoid, a probiotic or a protein fragment;
  • a general weight or blood-sugar ingredient, such as berberine, fiber or chromium, sold under a GLP-1 name;
  • nutrition support for people taking a GLP-1 drug, such as protein or vitamins. That is a different use, covered in our guide to supplements on GLP-1 drugs.

Why a small rise in your own GLP-1 is not Ozempic

The body's own GLP-1 is "rapidly degraded," with a half-life of 2 to 3 minutes. Semaglutide is engineered to resist that breakdown: the Ozempic label gives it a half-life "of approximately 1 week." A supplement that nudges natural GLP-1 up produces a brief, small signal. A weekly injection keeps a strong signal going all week.

The trials bear this out. In a 12-week trial in 103 adults with prediabetes, the lemon flavonoid extract Eriomin raised GLP-1 by 15% and lowered blood sugar by 5%, but "did not influence the anthropometric" measures, meaning weight and waist did not change.

Raising your own GLP-1 with food and eating habits

Your gut releases GLP-1 every time you eat, and a few small trials show that how you eat changes how much:

  • Protein before carbohydrate. In 15 adults with type 2 diabetes, a whey protein drink taken just before a high-sugar breakfast raised total GLP-1 by 141% and lowered blood sugar after the meal by 28%, compared with water. The trial was funded in part by Israel's Milk Council.
  • Vegetables and protein first, starch last. In 16 adults with type 2 diabetes, eating chicken and vegetables 10 minutes before bread and juice produced a higher GLP-1 response and a 53% smaller rise in blood sugar after the meal than eating the bread and juice first.
  • Eating slowly. In 17 healthy men, eating the same bowl of ice cream over 30 minutes instead of 5 produced a higher GLP-1 and PYY (another fullness hormone) response. Fullness ratings tended to be a little higher, but the difference was not statistically clear.

Each of these trials measured hormones and blood sugar after one meal. None tested whether the habit leads to weight loss over months, and none comes close to what the drugs do. They cost nothing to try, though, and they work through the same hormone that "GLP-1 booster" labels promise to raise.

The benchmark

The STEP 1 trial included 1,961 adults with overweight or obesity. Over 68 weeks, people taking weekly semaglutide lost an average of 14.9% of their body weight, compared with 2.4% for people taking a placebo. Both groups also got diet and exercise advice. For someone weighing 100 kg (220 lb), that is roughly 15 kg against 2.4 kg. That is about 12.7 kg more than placebo. Patches, drops and gummies

Patches. A 2026 analysis in a pharmacy journal examined 24 "natural GLP-1" patch products and one gel. Their most common ingredients were berberine, glutamine, cinnamon and pomegranate. The authors pointed out that US law defines a dietary supplement as something that is swallowed, "so all these products are illegal" as supplements. None posted a certificate of analysis, many lacked the required FDA disclaimer, and many used deceptive advertising. We found no trial showing that any of these ingredients works through the skin. More detail is in Do GLP-1 patches work?

Drops and gummies. We searched PubMed for trials of GLP-1 drops, sublingual products or gummies and found none. A drop or gummy can only hold a small amount of any ingredient, so check whether the dose matches what was used in that ingredient's studies. For most ingredients in the table, the studied doses were hundreds of milligrams to several grams a day.

How to check a GLP-1 drop or booster before you buy

Most searches for GLP-1 drops, oral liquids and boosters are for reviews of one product. We cannot test every product, but these checks work for any of them:

  1. Is the full ingredient panel published, with amounts? A "proprietary blend" or a missing panel means you cannot compare it with the research.
  2. Do the amounts match the trials? Compare the amount per serving with the amount used in the studies in the table above. A dropper or gummy usually holds far less.
  3. Does it claim to contain semaglutide, tirzepatide or retatrutide? Then it is not a supplement. The FDA has warned companies selling these drugs labeled "for research purposes" or "not for human consumption" directly to consumers, and says such products are of unknown quality and may be harmful.
  4. Does it promise results like Ozempic? No supplement has matched a GLP-1 drug in a trial, so that claim is not backed by evidence.
  5. Is there independent testing? A third-party testing seal or certificate of analysis shows that the contents match the label. It does not show that the product works.

Salmon peptides (salmon protein hydrolysate)

Salmon protein hydrolysate is salmon protein broken into small fragments, sold under the ingredient name ProGo and marketed in some products as a natural GLP-1 and GIP activator. Here is what has been published:

  • Laboratory study (2024). In cell tests, the hydrolysate raised GLP-1 and GIP receptor activity about 2.4-fold and 2.6-fold at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. This shows activity in a dish. It does not show what happens after digestion in a person. The authors were affiliated with the ingredient's maker, which sponsored the study.
  • Pilot trial (2026). Fourteen overweight adults took 12 g a day of the hydrolysate or whey protein for 56 days. The hydrolysate group had lower BMI and body fat as secondary outcomes. The authors describe the trial as small, exploratory and "hypothesis-generating," note that diet and activity were not recorded, and include company employees.
  • Separate trial of a different salmon protein (2020). In a trial of 93 overweight adults split into four groups, the group that took 2.5 g a day of salmon protein in tablets for 8 weeks saw no change in blood sugar or insulin. The authors wrote that it "did not affect glucose and insulin concentrations."

The supportive human data used 12 g a day of powder. We found no published trial of salmon peptide drops, and no trial comparing any salmon peptide product with a GLP-1 drug. Until larger independent trials exist, the claim that it works like a GLP-1 drug is not established.

Safety and who should be careful

  • Diabetes medication. Berberine, chromium, fiber and some probiotics can lower blood sugar. Combined with insulin or sulfonylureas, that can add up. Talk to your prescriber first.
  • On a GLP-1 drug already. No "GLP-1 booster" has been tested alongside semaglutide or tirzepatide.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. Most of these ingredients have not been tested; berberine in particular is advised against.
  • Fish allergy. Salmon peptide products are made from fish.
  • Product quality. Supplements do not have to prove they work, or prove what is in them, before sale.
  • Unapproved drugs. As of May 31, 2026, the FDA had received 990 reports of side effects or other problems in people using compounded semaglutide, and more than 730 for compounded tirzepatide. These reports do not prove the drug caused each problem.

Do not stop or replace a prescribed medication with a supplement without talking to your clinician.

Common questions

Do GLP-1 supplements work?

Not like the drugs. The best-supported ingredients had small effects in pooled trials: psyllium fiber led to about 2 kg more weight loss than a placebo, and berberine to about 0 to 2 kg, depending on the analysis. Semaglutide led to about 12% more weight loss than a placebo, or about 12 kg for a person who weighs 100 kg. Several popular ingredients show no meaningful effect.

What is the best natural GLP-1 supplement?

No head-to-head trials exist, so there is no evidence-based "best." Psyllium fiber and berberine have both been tested in pooled trials, but the effects were small: about 2 kg more weight loss than a placebo for psyllium, and about 0 to 2 kg for berberine, depending on the analysis.

Do GLP-1 patches work?

We found no trial of any GLP-1 patch. A 2026 analysis of 24 patch products concluded they cannot legally be sold as dietary supplements in the US, and found no quality certificates for any of them.

Do GLP-1 drops work?

We found no published trial of GLP-1 drops. Check whether the amount in a serving comes close to the dose used in that ingredient's studies.

Can you buy GLP-1 pills over the counter?

No. The approved GLP-1 pills, such as Wegovy tablets, Rybelsus and Foundayo, are prescription medicines. Products sold over the counter as "GLP-1 pills" are supplements that contain no GLP-1 drug. See our guide to oral GLP-1 pills.

Do GLP-1 probiotics work?

The evidence is two small trials. In a small three-group trial that 32 adults finished, a pasteurized Akkermansia product improved insulin sensitivity, but its 2.3 kg weight difference from placebo was not statistically significant. In a three-group trial of 76 people with type 2 diabetes, a five-strain product lowered HbA1c by about 0.6 points more than placebo. Both were run by researchers with ties to the products, and neither showed meaningful weight loss.

Do GLP-1 boosters have any benefits?

Some ingredients used in boosters had small effects in pooled trials: about 2 kg more weight loss than a placebo for psyllium fiber, about 0 to 2 kg for berberine depending on the analysis, or slightly lower blood sugar. That is the realistic upper end. The finished booster products themselves have not been shown to work like a GLP-1 drug.

Are GLP-1 boosters safe?

The main risks are lower blood sugar if you take diabetes medication, untested combinations with GLP-1 drugs, and products that do not contain what the label says. Products claiming to contain semaglutide or tirzepatide are unapproved drugs, not supplements.

What about "GLP-1 6-in-1" oral liquids?

These are drops sold under another name. Use the same checks: a full ingredient panel with amounts, amounts that match the research, and no claim to contain a GLP-1 drug or to work like one.

Can you raise GLP-1 naturally?

Yes, modestly and briefly. Eating releases GLP-1, and in small trials eating protein or vegetables before starch, or eating more slowly, raised the GLP-1 response to a meal. A lemon flavonoid extract raised it by 15 to 17% in two trials. Natural GLP-1 lasts only minutes, and none of these has been shown to cause weight loss.

Is there a natural Ozempic?

No. Nothing sold as a supplement has matched semaglutide in a trial, and none has been compared with it directly.

Can I take a GLP-1 supplement with Ozempic or Wegovy?

None has been tested in combination. Ingredients that lower blood sugar could add to the drug's effect, so ask your prescriber.
