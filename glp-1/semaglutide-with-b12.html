Semaglutide with B12: what the evidence shows

Short answer. "Semaglutide with B12" is a compounded injection that mixes semaglutide with vitamin B12 (often the form called cyanocobalamin). It is not an FDA-approved drug, and Ozempic and Wegovy do not contain B12. We found no clinical trial testing whether the added B12 improves weight loss, energy or nausea compared with semaglutide alone.

B12 itself is a safe vitamin, and some people on these drugs do need to watch their B12 levels. The open questions are about the compounded product: nobody has tested the combination, and the FDA does not review its safety, strength or quality.

What "semaglutide with B12" is

Ozempic and Wegovy are the FDA-approved semaglutide injections. Their labels list semaglutide plus a short set of inactive ingredients: a phosphate buffer, water, and either salt or preservatives depending on the pen. Neither lists vitamin B12.

Semaglutide with B12 comes from compounding pharmacies, often through telehealth companies. A survey published in 2026 by pharmacy researchers at the University of Connecticut searched compounding websites and found 33 semaglutide or tirzepatide products that differed from the approved drugs. Nearly half combined the drug with some mix of cyanocobalamin, glycine, niacinamide, docusate or ondansetron. The authors explain why: once the drug shortage ended, compounders "can only legally sell unique products", and adding an ingredient is one way to make a product different.

Their conclusion was direct: "We found little justification for adding nutrients" to these injections, and they called the products' popularity concerning "given the lack of evidence for safety and efficacy".

Weight loss

We searched PubMed on November 10, 2026 for clinical trials of semaglutide combined with B12 and found none. The one study of the combination is a look back at records from a commercial wellness studio: 94 people who received weekly compounded semaglutide with cyanocobalamin lost an average of 4.57% of their body weight in three months. The study had no comparison group, so it cannot show what the B12 contributed, if anything.

Energy

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements addresses this directly: manufacturers often promote B12 for energy and endurance, "however, vitamin B12 doesn't provide these benefits in people who get enough B12 from their diet." A meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials in 6,276 people without overt B12 deficiency found no effect of B12 supplements on cognitive function or depressive symptoms, and too little data to judge fatigue.

Nausea

We found no human study showing that B12 mixed into a semaglutide injection reduces nausea. The closest research is a 2020 laboratory study in which scientists chemically attached a precursor of vitamin B12 to a different GLP-1 drug. In shrews, that new compound caused far less vomiting. It is a different molecule made by bonding the two together, which is not what happens when B12 is added to a vial.

Preventing deficiency

This claim has some basis, though not for the product itself. People eat less on GLP-1 drugs, and two 2026 reviews list B12 among the nutrients that may run low over time. Between them they note that the evidence is mostly observational, that most abnormalities reported so far are subclinical, and that cause and effect is not established. A 2026 case report described severe nerve damage with low thiamine, folate and B12 in a woman who had months of vomiting on semaglutide; she improved after vitamin treatment.

Whether the B12 in a compounded injection prevents any of this has not been studied. In the wellness-studio study, the weekly B12 dose ranged from 0.125 mg to 0.24 mg.

Who should pay attention to B12

Most people in the United States get enough B12 from food, according to the NIH. The groups it lists as more likely to run low include:

  • People taking metformin. Metformin can reduce B12 absorption. A meta-analysis of six randomized trials found lower B12 levels with metformin, more so at higher doses. Many people on semaglutide for diabetes also take metformin.
  • People taking acid-reducing drugs such as omeprazole.
  • Older adults, and people who eat little or no animal food.
  • People who have had weight-loss or stomach surgery.

A blood test can check B12 status. If you are in one of these groups, or you have had prolonged vomiting or very low food intake on a GLP-1 drug, ask your clinician about testing. Tingling or numbness in the hands and feet, unusual tiredness or weakness, or confusion are symptoms the NIH links to B12 deficiency and are reasons to seek care promptly.

A confirmed deficiency is treated with B12 itself, by mouth or by prescription injection, at doses a clinician chooses. That does not require a combined semaglutide product.

Is B12 itself risky?

No. The NIH states that "vitamin B12 has not been shown to cause any harm, even at high doses." The concerns below are about compounded semaglutide products, not about the vitamin.

Is it the same as Ozempic or Wegovy?

No. The FDA states that compounded drugs "are not FDA approved", which means "the agency does not review compounded drugs for safety, effectiveness or quality before they are marketed." It lists a claim that "the compounded drug is the same as an FDA-approved drug" as a warning sign when buying from a telehealth company.

The FDA's published concerns about compounded semaglutide include:

  • Dosing errors. The agency has received reports of adverse events, some requiring hospitalization, linked to patients measuring the wrong dose from a vial and to clinicians miscalculating doses.
  • Salt forms. Some compounded products use semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate, which the FDA says are different active ingredients from the approved drug.
  • Storage and shipping. The FDA advises not using an injectable GLP-1 drug that arrives warm.
  • Adverse event reports. As of May 31, 2026, the FDA had received 990 reports involving compounded semaglutide, and notes that such events are likely underreported. It also notes that many resemble the side effects of the approved drugs, and that a report does not prove the product caused the event.

An analysis of the FDA's adverse event database from 2018 to 2024 found 707 reports involving compounded GLP-1 drugs out of 81,078. Compared with reports for approved products, they were more likely to mention preparation errors, contamination and hospitalization, and less likely to mention dosing errors. Databases like this show patterns in what gets reported, not how often problems occur.

One study bears on B12 specifically. In 2026, scientists at Eli Lilly, which makes the approved tirzepatide products, tested compounded tirzepatide with B12 and reported a previously unknown impurity formed by a chemical reaction between the drug and certain forms of B12. Its clinical effects are unknown. The study concerned tirzepatide, not semaglutide, and its authors work for a company that competes with compounders. It still shows why a mixture needs testing before it is assumed to be stable.

If you use a compounded product

The FDA says compounded drugs should be used only when a patient's medical needs cannot be met by an FDA-approved drug. For people who do use them, its guidance includes:

  • Get a prescription from your doctor and fill it at a state-licensed pharmacy.
  • Ask your prescriber or the compounder exactly how to measure your dose.
  • Discard a multi-dose vial 28 days after first use, even if medication remains.
  • Use a new sterile needle and syringe for every dose.
  • Do not use a product that arrives warm or without adequate cooling.

Common questions

Does B12 make semaglutide work better?

No trial has tested that. The only study of the combination had no comparison group.

Does Ozempic or Wegovy contain B12?

No. Neither label lists vitamin B12 among its ingredients.

Is semaglutide with B12 FDA approved?

No. It is a compounded product. The FDA does not review compounded drugs for safety, effectiveness or quality before they are sold.

Does B12 help with semaglutide nausea?

We found no human evidence that it does.

Does semaglutide cause B12 deficiency?

It has not been shown to cause it directly. Low B12 has been reported in some people on GLP-1 drugs, mostly in observational studies, and the risk is higher with metformin, prolonged vomiting or very low food intake.

Should I take B12 while on semaglutide?

That depends on your B12 status and risk factors, which a blood test and your clinician can assess. B12 has not been shown to cause harm, but it is not known to help people who are not low in it.
