GLP-1 vitamins and supplements: what has evidence

Short answer. We searched PubMed for randomized trials of creatine, protein supplements, fiber, magnesium, biotin, collagen and berberine in people taking semaglutide or tirzepatide, and found none. Everything below is borrowed from other groups of people, such as older adults doing resistance training or adults with chronic constipation.

The same is true for vitamins. We found no published randomized trial of any vitamin, mineral or multivitamin taken alongside these drugs. What the evidence does show is narrower: studies that track people on GLP-1 drugs find that many eat less of several nutrients, and some are diagnosed with deficiencies, most often of vitamin D. Those studies show a link, not that the drugs cause the deficiency.

That borrowed evidence is reasonable for a few uses and absent for most products sold as "GLP-1 support". Nothing here replaces advice from the clinician who prescribes your medication.

Why supplements come up at all

GLP-1 drugs cause large weight loss. In the STEP 1 trial, adults taking semaglutide 2.4 mg lost an average of 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks, against 2.4% with placebo. In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide produced average losses of 15.0% to 20.9% over 72 weeks, depending on dose, against 3.1% with placebo.

Two things follow from that. Some of the weight lost is lean tissue, and the drugs commonly cause stomach and bowel side effects. In the trials summarized on the Wegovy label, adults taking semaglutide 2.4 mg reported:

There is also a simpler reason. Smaller meals and less food mean fewer vitamins and minerals coming in. A 2025 expert advisory warns that this large, rapid drop in eating "can lead to insufficient intakes of essential vitamins and minerals."

Most supplement questions trace back to one of these: vitamins, muscle, constipation or hair.

Which nutrients run low: what studies found

Two kinds of studies have looked at this. Neither was a randomized trial, so they show what goes along with GLP-1 treatment, not what the drug itself causes.

  • Diagnosed deficiencies. A study of insurance claims followed 461,382 adults who started a GLP-1 drug between 2017 and 2021; about 80% had type 2 diabetes. Within 12 months, 22.4% had been diagnosed with at least one nutritional deficiency. Vitamin D deficiency was the most common, at 13.6%. In a smaller matched comparison of 4,505 GLP-1 users with 4,505 people taking metformin alone, deficiencies were somewhat more common in the GLP-1 group at 12 months (18.6% against 16.5%; vitamin D 10.9% against 9.2%). Iron deficiency anemia was about the same in both groups. The study was funded by a company that makes nutrition products, and most authors work for it. The authors note that it could not show cause and effect, and it had no information on supplement use.
  • What people eat. A cross-sectional study asked 69 adults taking a GLP-1 drug to log three days of food. On average, their diets fell short of recommended intakes for fiber, calcium, iron, magnesium, potassium, choline and vitamins A, C, D and E. Almost all of them (98.6%) got less vitamin D from their diet than recommended. The study was small, used self-reported food logs, recruited whoever signed up, had no comparison group, and was funded by a supplement retailer that employs all its authors.

What is not known: how often these gaps cause symptoms or harm, how much more common they are than in similar people not taking these drugs (the one comparison, with metformin users, found small differences), and whether any supplement prevents them. The large claims study ended in 2021, before tirzepatide was in use, so it says little about tirzepatide.

Do you need a multivitamin?

No study has answered this yet. We found no published randomized trial of a multivitamin in people taking semaglutide or tirzepatide. Multivitamins marketed as "GLP-1 vitamins" have not been tested in a trial either. One placebo-controlled trial of a daily multivitamin in GLP-1 users is registered (NCT07234916; 246 adults planned, 12 months, measuring blood levels of B12, vitamin D and ferritin), but we found no published results.

The best guidance available is the 2025 joint advisory from the American College of Lifestyle Medicine, the American Society for Nutrition, the Obesity Medicine Association and The Obesity Society. It is expert opinion based on reviewing the evidence, not a trial. On nutrients, it says:

  • Which nutrients to watch. It lists "iron, calcium, magnesium, zinc, and vitamins A, D, E, K, B1, B12, and C" as examples of nutrients of concern when food intake falls a lot.
  • Supplements. "Dietary supplements can be proactively considered for at-risk nutrients, such as vitamin D, calcium, B12, or a multivitamin-mineral tablet, at appropriate doses and tailored to each person's needs." That is an option for clinicians to weigh for each person, not a statement that everyone on these drugs needs one.
  • Testing. It says "additional assessment and/or laboratory testing may be indicated prior to therapy" for people with recent or current use of a very low-calorie diet, past bariatric surgery, celiac disease, other inflammatory conditions that make deficiency more likely, or a past nutrient deficiency.
  • Muscle and bone. It names resistance training and diet as the way to protect muscle and bone, and notes that low protein intake from reduced appetite may add to muscle loss. See the protein section below.

Vitamin B12

We found no evidence that semaglutide or tirzepatide lowers B12 directly. The clearest known risk is a different drug that many people with type 2 diabetes also take: metformin. In a long-term follow-up of the Diabetes Prevention Program, a randomized trial, low B12 was more common with metformin than with placebo at 5 years (4.3% against 2.3%). Each year of metformin use was linked to about 13% higher odds of B12 deficiency (a relative measure). The authors suggested that doctors consider routine B12 testing in people taking metformin.

The NIH also lists older adults, people who have had some types of stomach or intestinal surgery, people with celiac or Crohn's disease, people who eat little or no animal foods, and people taking acid-reducing drugs such as omeprazole as more likely to run low. We cover B12 in more detail, including compounded "semaglutide with B12" products, in semaglutide with B12.

Vitamin D and calcium

The concern here is bone. The 2025 advisory notes that "rapid weight reduction with GLP-1s or other therapies can also affect bone density," and says people "should be assessed for risk of sarcopenia and osteopenia" (low muscle and low bone mass).

We found two randomized trials that measured bone density by DXA scan:

  • Semaglutide. In a 52-week phase 2 trial of 64 adults at higher risk of fracture, mostly women past menopause, semaglutide 1.0 mg weekly lowered bone density at the total hip and lower spine compared with placebo. There was no difference at the femoral neck (top of the thigh bone). Weight was 6.8 kg lower with semaglutide. This was a small trial at a lower dose than Wegovy.
  • Liraglutide. In a 52-week trial of 195 adults who had first lost weight on an 8-week low-calorie diet, liraglutide alone lowered bone density at the hip and spine more than exercise alone. People who took liraglutide and also exercised kept bone density no different from the placebo group.

Neither trial tested vitamin D or calcium, and neither measured fractures. Whether these supplements protect bone in people on GLP-1 drugs is not known. What the studies above add is that vitamin D deficiency was the most common deficiency diagnosed in the large claims study, and that diets logged by GLP-1 users were low in both vitamin D and calcium. For general evidence on the vitamin, see our vitamin D evidence page.

Iron and other minerals

The evidence here is thin. The advisory lists iron, calcium, magnesium and zinc among nutrients of concern. The 69-person food-log study found average intakes below recommendations for iron, calcium, magnesium and potassium. In the large claims study, 3.2% of GLP-1 users were diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia within 12 months, and in the matched comparison the rate was about the same as in people taking metformin alone (2.8% against 3.0%).

We found no trial of iron or any other mineral supplement in people on these drugs. Magnesium for constipation is covered below.

Protein and muscle: food first, powder as a backup

How much lean tissue is lost

In a body-composition substudy of SURMOUNT-1 (160 participants scanned by DXA), about 75% of the weight lost was fat and about 25% was lean mass. The split was about the same in the tirzepatide and placebo groups, though only 36 people were in the placebo group.

A 2026 systematic review in Annals of Internal Medicine covered 35 randomized trials of incretin drugs. The median share of weight loss coming from muscle-related measures was 28.3%, with a wide range between studies. The reviewers noted that no trial reported objective measures of physical function, so it is not yet known whether this loss affects strength or mobility.

What expert groups recommend

A 2025 joint advisory from four US lifestyle medicine, nutrition and obesity societies names "preserving muscle and bone mass through resistance training and appropriate diet" as a priority during GLP-1 treatment, along with preventing nutrient deficiencies when appetite and food intake fall. Protein targets depend on body size, age and kidney function, so they are best set with a clinician or dietitian. Protein powders are a convenience for people who cannot eat enough; we found no trial testing them in people on these drugs.

In the 69-person food-log study described above, average protein intake was about 77 g a day. The authors judged that this fell short of needs once body weight was taken into account. That study was small and funded by a supplement retailer, and it measured food intake only.

Creatine

Creatine has not been tested in a randomized trial in people taking GLP-1 drugs. The case for it is indirect: a meta-analysis of 22 randomized trials in 721 older adults found that creatine taken during resistance training added about 1.4 kg more lean tissue than training with placebo, with modest strength gains. Two limits matter. The benefit was shown with resistance training, not instead of it. And the participants were not losing weight on medication. See our creatine evidence page for safety context. If you are watching the scale, see whether creatine makes you gain weight.

Constipation: fiber and magnesium have evidence, in other populations

Neither has been studied for constipation caused by GLP-1 drugs. Both have trial evidence in adults with chronic constipation:

  • Fiber. A meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials (1,251 adults) found that fiber supplements helped. In the trials that counted how many people improved, 66% improved on fiber against 41% on control. Psyllium was among the fibers with a clear effect, and benefit appeared only at doses above 10 g a day.
  • Magnesium oxide. In a small placebo-controlled trial (34 women, 1.5 g a day for 28 days), 70.6% improved on magnesium oxide against 25.0% on placebo.

The 2023 joint guideline from the American Gastroenterological Association and American College of Gastroenterology gives conditional recommendations for fiber and magnesium oxide in chronic constipation, and a strong recommendation for polyethylene glycol, an over-the-counter laxative that is not a supplement.

Cautions. Fiber can add to bloating, which GLP-1 drugs already cause in some people. Magnesium is cleared by the kidneys, so people with reduced kidney function should not use magnesium laxatives without medical advice. Severe or persistent constipation, or constipation with vomiting and abdominal pain, needs prompt medical attention, not a supplement. More on forms and safety is on our magnesium evidence page.

Hair shedding: no supplement has been shown to help

A 2026 systematic review of 24 studies, mostly drug-safety reports and observational studies rather than randomized trials, found hair loss reported more often with semaglutide and tirzepatide than with older GLP-1 drugs. Rapid weight loss may be a contributor, and telogen effluvium, a temporary shedding that follows physical stress, was one of the main types reported.

Biotin is the supplement most often marketed for this. A systematic review of biotin for hair and nails found only 18 reported cases of benefit, and every one involved a person with an underlying deficiency or medical cause. Evidence in otherwise healthy people is limited. We found no study of biotin or collagen for hair shedding during GLP-1 treatment.

Supplements that need care with GLP-1 drugs

  • Anything that lowers blood sugar. Berberine lowers glucose in people with type 2 diabetes. The Wegovy label warns of low blood sugar when semaglutide is combined with insulin or sulfonylureas. No trial has tested berberine together with a GLP-1 drug, so tell your prescriber before combining them, especially if you take other diabetes medication. See berberine vs Ozempic.
  • Anything taken by mouth on a schedule. The label notes that semaglutide delays stomach emptying and may affect the absorption of oral medications. Whether that changes how any supplement is absorbed has not been studied.
  • Laxative herbs. Diarrhea and vomiting are already common on these drugs, and the label warns that the resulting fluid loss can injure the kidneys. Stimulant laxative herbs such as senna can add to fluid loss.

What we found no evidence for

  • Electrolyte products for people on GLP-1 drugs. We found no clinical study. Staying hydrated matters when vomiting or diarrhea occurs, but that is not evidence for a specific product.
  • "GLP-1 support" blends and supplements claimed to raise the body's own GLP-1. We found no randomized trial of any such product alongside these drugs.
  • Collagen for muscle or hair during GLP-1 treatment. Not studied.
  • Multivitamins sold as "GLP-1 vitamins". We found no trial of any such product.

Vitamin B12 mixed into compounded semaglutide is covered separately: see semaglutide with B12. Drops, patches and "boosters" sold as natural alternatives to the drugs are reviewed in do GLP-1 supplements work?

Questions to bring to your prescriber

These are questions to ask, not a list of things to take. Which ones matter depends on your health, your diet and your other medicines.

  • Given my diet and health history, would checking any nutrient levels before or during treatment be useful?
  • I take metformin (or an acid-reducing drug, or I eat little animal food). Should my vitamin B12 be checked?
  • Should my vitamin D level be checked, and is my bone health something to watch while I lose weight?
  • Is an iron or ferritin test worth doing for me, especially if I have had low iron before?
  • I have had bariatric surgery, celiac disease or a past deficiency. Does that change what we monitor?
  • How much protein should I aim for, given my weight, age and kidney function? Could a dietitian help?
  • I already take a supplement. Could it interact with my GLP-1 drug or my other medicines?

Common questions

Should I take creatine on semaglutide or tirzepatide?

No trial answers this directly. Creatine adds lean tissue when combined with resistance training in older adults, so it is a reasonable question to raise with your clinician if you are doing strength training. It is not a substitute for training or adequate protein.

How much of the weight lost on GLP-1 drugs is muscle?

In trials that measured it, roughly a quarter of weight lost came from lean mass, with wide variation between studies. In SURMOUNT-1 the proportion was the same for tirzepatide and placebo.

Which magnesium is used for constipation?

The trial evidence and the gastroenterology guideline are for magnesium oxide. People with kidney disease should ask a clinician first.

Does biotin stop hair loss on Ozempic or Wegovy?

There is no evidence that it does. Published benefit from biotin is limited to people with a deficiency or an underlying condition.

Can I take berberine with a GLP-1 drug?

The combination has not been tested in a trial. Both lower blood sugar, so check with your prescriber first.

What vitamins should I take on a GLP-1 drug?

There is no proven list, because no published randomized trial has tested a vitamin in people taking these drugs. A 2025 expert advisory names vitamin D, calcium and B12 as nutrients a clinician may consider supplementing for people at risk, tailored to each person. Whether any apply to you depends on your diet, other medicines and blood tests, so ask your prescriber.

Do I need a multivitamin on Ozempic or Wegovy?

No one knows yet. No published trial has tested a multivitamin in people taking semaglutide. The 2025 advisory lists a multivitamin-mineral tablet as one option clinicians can consider for people at risk of low intake, not as something everyone needs. Your prescriber can look at your diet and test results with you.

Can semaglutide cause low B12?

We found no evidence that semaglutide lowers B12 directly. Metformin, which many people with diabetes take alongside semaglutide, is linked to lower B12 in long-term trial follow-up. If you take metformin, ask your prescriber whether a B12 test makes sense.

Should I take vitamin D on tirzepatide?

No published trial has tested vitamin D in people taking tirzepatide. Weight loss on GLP-1 drugs can lower bone density, and vitamin D deficiency was the most common deficiency diagnosed in a large records study of GLP-1 users, but neither finding shows that a supplement helps. That study ended before tirzepatide was in use. Your prescriber can tell you whether your level is worth checking.

Are "GLP-1 vitamins" the same as GLP-1 supplements?

Not quite. "GLP-1 vitamins" usually means vitamins or multivitamins sold to people already on a GLP-1 drug, while "GLP-1 supplements" are usually sold as boosters or natural alternatives to the drugs. We found no trial of either kind of product. Our review of patches, drops and boosters covers the second group: see do GLP-1 supplements work?

Is protein powder worth it on a GLP-1 drug?

No trial has tested protein powder in people taking these drugs. Expert groups stress resistance training and an appropriate diet to help protect muscle during weight loss, and low protein intake may add to muscle loss. Powder is one way to add protein for people who cannot eat enough from meals. A clinician or dietitian can help you judge how much protein you need.

Why do I feel dizzy or tired on a GLP-1 drug?

Both are listed side effects. In the trials on the Wegovy label, 8% of adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg reported dizziness and 11% fatigue, against 4% and 5% on placebo. On the Zepbound label, the figures for tirzepatide 15 mg were 4% and 7%, against 2% and 3%. Low blood pressure was reported in 1.3% of people on Wegovy and 1.6% on Zepbound, against 0.4% and 0.1% on placebo, more often in people also taking blood pressure medicine, and the labels link some cases to fluid lost through vomiting or diarrhea. Low blood sugar is another possible cause, mainly in people with type 2 diabetes who also take insulin or a sulfonylurea. These rates come from randomized trials (grade A for how often they happen), but no trial has tested what helps. Fainting, dizziness that does not pass, or passing very little urine is worth telling your prescriber about promptly.

Do I need electrolytes on a GLP-1 drug?

We found no study of electrolyte drinks or powders in people taking these drugs, so there is no evidence either way for a product (grade C). What the labels do say is that nausea, vomiting and diarrhea can dehydrate you, and that dehydration has been followed by kidney injury, sometimes needing dialysis, in reports after the drugs went on sale. The Zepbound label tells prescribers to advise patients to take precautions to avoid fluid loss. A 2025 expert advisory suggests drinking adequate fluids and says people with kidney problems should be counseled on preventing dehydration. Whether you need anything beyond water and food, for example during days of vomiting or diarrhea, is a question for your prescriber.

What if I can barely eat on a GLP-1 drug?

Eating less is how these drugs work, but very low intake brings its own problems. A 2025 expert advisory cites studies measuring 16% to 39% lower calorie intake on these drugs and warns that this can mean too few vitamins and minerals, especially below about 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,800 for men. It suggests small meals every 3 to 4 hours, eating protein-rich foods first and drinking adequate fluids. That is expert opinion: no trial has tested these steps in people on the drugs (grade C). The advisory lists fatigue beyond what is expected, unusual hair loss, muscle weakness and poor wound healing as possible signs of a deficiency, so mention any of these to your prescriber, who can also refer you to a dietitian.

Which fiber works best for constipation: psyllium, prunes or kiwifruit?

No trial has compared them in people taking GLP-1 drugs. In adults with chronic constipation, a small 3-week crossover trial (40 people) found more complete bowel movements with prunes than with psyllium, while a partly randomized 4-week trial of 79 people found kiwifruit, prunes and psyllium helped a similar share of people, with the fewest side effects on kiwifruit. A 2024 pooled analysis found fruit, kiwifruit in particular, added about one bowel movement every three weeks more than psyllium, and no difference between prunes and psyllium (grade B: small, short trials with mixed results). Fiber can add to the bloating these drugs already cause.

Is it safe to take a laxative or magnesium every day for months?

For polyethylene glycol (PEG 3350), an over-the-counter laxative the gastroenterology guideline strongly recommends, one trial followed 153 adults for a year: bowel function stayed improved and side effects were mostly mild stomach complaints, such as abdominal pain in 4.5% and diarrhea in 3.8% (grade B: one open-label extension, run by the maker, using a version with added electrolytes). For magnesium oxide, reviews and case reports describe dangerously high blood magnesium, mostly in older people and people with reduced kidney function (grade C). Neither has been studied in people taking GLP-1 drugs, so long-term use is worth raising with your prescriber.

When does constipation on a GLP-1 drug need urgent care?

Constipation is common: on the Wegovy label it was reported by 24% of adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg against 11% on placebo. Severe stomach and bowel reactions were reported by 4.1% against 0.9%, and reports after the drug went on sale include ileus (the bowel stopping moving), intestinal blockage and severe constipation with impacted stool; those reports cannot show how often this happens or prove the drug caused it. The label tells prescribers to have patients contact them about severe or persistent stomach or bowel symptoms. Not passing stool or gas, a swollen or painful belly, or vomiting alongside constipation are reasons to get medical care promptly rather than adding a supplement.

How long does nausea last on a GLP-1 drug?

For most people it is worst while the dose is being raised and fades after that. In the trials on the Wegovy label, 44% of adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg reported nausea, against 16% on placebo. On the Zepbound label, the figure for tirzepatide 15 mg was 28%, against 8%, and the label says most nausea, vomiting and diarrhea happened during dose escalation and decreased over time. A pooled analysis of the STEP 1 to 3 trials found that 98.1% of stomach side effects on semaglutide were mild to moderate, that they were transient and came mostly during or shortly after dose escalation, and that 4.3% of people stopped the drug because of them. A 2022 expert consensus says an episode of nausea usually clears within about 8 days of starting. These rates come from randomized trials (grade A for how often it happens). Vomiting that will not stop or not being able to keep fluids down is worth telling your prescriber about promptly.

Does ginger help nausea on a GLP-1 drug?

No one has tested it. A PubMed search on November 25, 2026 found no study of ginger in people taking these drugs (grade C for this use). Ginger has been tested for other kinds of nausea. A 2026 umbrella review of 18 systematic reviews found that ginger reduced nausea in pregnancy compared with placebo, although most of those reviews were of low methodological quality. A 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials in chemotherapy found that ginger capsules taken with standard anti-nausea medicine reduced severe nausea. Nausea in pregnancy or chemotherapy has different causes, so those results may not carry over. A 2022 expert consensus on these drugs lists ginger root or ginger drinks among foods that may ease nausea, alongside eating slowly, eating smaller portions, stopping when you feel full and avoiding strong smells. That is expert opinion, not a trial result. If you are thinking about a ginger supplement, ask your pharmacist whether it fits with your other medicines.

Why don't GLP-1 drugs work for some people?

Most people in the trials lost a meaningful amount, but not everyone. In STEP 1, 86.4% of adults without diabetes on semaglutide 2.4 mg lost at least 5% of their weight over 68 weeks, so about 1 in 7 did not. In SURMOUNT-1, 85% to 91% of people on tirzepatide reached 5%, depending on dose (grade A). People with type 2 diabetes tend to lose less: the average was 9.6% on semaglutide 2.4 mg in STEP 2, against 14.9% in STEP 1, though these were separate trials. In the SELECT trial of adults with heart disease, baseline measures such as age, medical history and lab tests explained less than 10% of the difference in how much people lost. Women lost more than men (a median of 16.6% against 12.4%), and people whose highest dose was the lowest step lost a median 6.0%, against 13.3% for those who reached 2.4 mg (grade B, an analysis within a trial). A 2026 review concludes that no single trait reliably predicts who will respond and that early weight change on treatment is the most useful guide so far. If you are losing little, your prescriber can review your treatment with you.

Can a GLP-1 drug cause vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency?

It appears to be rare, and the published cases followed long-lasting vomiting or very low food intake. A 2026 systematic review found six published case reports of Wernicke's encephalopathy, a brain disorder caused by lack of thiamine, in people taking semaglutide for obesity. All six had prolonged stomach symptoms and substantial weight loss first, and outcomes were poor in several, including lasting memory problems or death. A 2026 study of the FDA's adverse event reports found 15 cases linked to GLP-1 drugs, mostly semaglutide or tirzepatide, and these were reported more often than with other medicines. Case reports and adverse event reports cannot show how often this happens or prove that the drug caused it (grade C). No trial has tested thiamine supplements in people on these drugs, and the 2025 advisory lists B1 among the nutrients of concern when food intake falls a lot. Confusion, unsteady walking, or new double vision or eye movement problems, especially after days of vomiting or barely eating, need urgent medical care.

What is "Ozempic face," and can a supplement fix it?

"Ozempic face" is a popular name, not a diagnosis, for a hollow or saggy look in the face after weight loss on these drugs. A 2026 review notes that similar changes happen after other large weight loss, so it is not known to be an effect of the drug itself. In one study of 20 patients with head scans before and after starting a GLP-1 drug, midface volume fell by a median 9%, or about 7% for every 10 kg lost. In a survey of 504 users run by a cosmetics company, 38.7% reported skin changes, most often facial volume loss (10.9%) and sagging (8.3%), and these were more common in people over 55 and in those who lost more than 20 pounds. That is grade C evidence, from small studies without comparison groups. No supplement has been tested for it: a 2026 systematic review found only four uncontrolled studies of any treatment, testing fillers, a radiofrequency device and a skin cream, and said they were too weak to show what works. Collagen supplements have not been tested in people on these drugs, and in general skin trials their benefit disappeared in higher-quality and non-industry-funded studies (see our collagen evidence). Copper peptide (GHK-Cu) creams have not been tested for it either. A dermatologist or facial plastic surgeon can talk through options.

Do magnesium, omega-3, vitamin K2, glutathione or NAD interact with GLP-1 drugs?

No study has tested any of them together with semaglutide or tirzepatide; we found none in a PubMed search on November 25, 2026 (grade C). The Wegovy and Zepbound labels name none of them. They do say the drugs slow stomach emptying and may change how other medicines taken by mouth are absorbed, and they ask you to tell your prescriber about every vitamin and herbal supplement you take. The Wegovy tablet label says to wait at least 30 minutes after the tablet before eating, drinking or taking other oral medicines. Some of these supplements matter for other medicines: NIH fact sheets say vitamin K can interfere with warfarin, fish oil might add to warfarin's effect though most studies found no significant change, and magnesium can reduce absorption of some antibiotics and osteoporosis drugs. For glutathione, the only report we found was one case of a severe inflammatory reaction after a high-dose glutathione infusion in a person self-administering tirzepatide; one case cannot show cause. Berberine is covered above.

Why do I get sulfur burps on a GLP-1 drug, and does anything help?

Burping is a listed side effect. In the trials on the Wegovy label, 7% of adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg reported burping, against under 1% on placebo; on the Zepbound label the figures were 4% to 5% on tirzepatide, against 1%. The labels say these drugs slow stomach emptying, but neither mentions a sulfur or rotten-egg smell, and a PubMed search on November 25, 2026 found no study of sulfur burps, or of any supplement for them, in people taking these drugs (grade C). Both labels warn about pancreatitis, which can show up as persistent or severe stomach pain, so burping with that kind of pain or with vomiting that will not stop needs medical attention, not a supplement.

Which fiber fits a low-carb or gluten-free diet on a GLP-1 drug?

No trial has tested fiber supplements in people on low-carb or gluten-free diets who take these drugs; we found none in a PubMed search on November 25, 2026 (grade C). Psyllium, a fiber with good trial evidence for constipation in other adults, comes from the seed husk of the Plantago ovata plant, not from a grain. Fiber products can contain other ingredients, so check the label if you avoid gluten. Fiber can add to the bloating these drugs already cause.
