Does creatine make you gain weight? What the studies actually show

Short answer. Creatine can raise scale weight, and early increases often include total body water. DXA lean-mass estimates can rise when water rises, so a quick change is not automatically new muscle. Longer resistance-training reviews report lean-mass differences, but the trials reviewed here do not show that the early weight change is fat gain.

Frequently asked questions

Does creatine make you gain weight?
It can raise scale weight. In a 28-day trial, creatine increased muscle creatine, body mass, and total body water. In a 7-day soccer-player study, average total body water rose 2.3 L and body weight rose 1.0 kg versus placebo. [PMIDs 12937471, 26509366]

Is that weight fat?
The trials reviewed here do not show that the early weight change is fat gain. Early increases often include body water, and DXA “lean mass” estimates can rise when water rises. [PMIDs 12937471, 36960692]

Why can the scale change quickly?
A short creatine period can increase total body water. In one trial, the split between extracellular and intracellular water did not change even as total body water and body mass rose. [PMID 12937471]

Does later weight include lean mass?
Creatine plus resistance training increased lean body mass by 1.10 kg in a systematic review; without exercise the change was 0.03 kg and was not statistically significant. A 7-day creatine period also raised DXA lean-mass estimates before a training program began. [PMIDs 35986981, 40292479]

Do results look the same in women?
Not every result is the same. The lean-mass review found a 1.46 kg increase in males and a 0.29 kg change in females that was not statistically significant. A small study in female dancers found higher total body water and DXA lean-mass estimates. [PMIDs 35986981, 36960692]

What amounts did studies use?
Protocols included 25 g/day for 7 days then 5 g/day, 0.3 g/kg/day for 7 days, and 5 g/day in a 13-week trial. Those are research protocols, not personal instructions. [PMIDs 12937471, 26509366, 40292479]

How to read the evidence

  • Grade A: a published Phase 3 randomized trial, or for nutrient pages a published systematic review of randomized trials, for outcomes reported within that evidence.
  • Grade B: a Phase 2 trial, exploratory or subgroup analysis, small human trial, observational study, or labeling statement beyond a trial abstract.
  • Grade C: uncontrolled reports, animal or laboratory inference, mechanism-based predictions, and comparisons between separate trials.

Early changes: body water and scale weight

Powers and colleagues studied 16 men and 16 women involved in resistance training. The study used 25 g/day for 7 days, then 5 g/day for 21 days, versus placebo. Creatine increased muscle creatine, body mass, and total body water, while the split between extracellular and intracellular water did not change. Grade B.

Deminice and colleagues randomized 13 under-20 soccer players. With 0.3 g/kg/day for 7 days, total body water increased by 2.3 ± 1.0 L and body weight by 1.0 ± 0.4 kg versus placebo. Grade B.

Why DXA lean mass needs context

In a 42-day trial of 13 female collegiate dancers, total body water in the seven who took creatine rose from 32.2 ± 3.5 kg to 32.7 ± 3.6 kg while DXA lean-mass estimates rose from 39.8 ± 3.6 kg to 41.5 ± 4.5 kg. Grade B. A higher DXA estimate alongside more water does not settle whether the change is muscle.

A wash-in period separated early change from training

Desai and colleagues randomized 63 participants to 5 g/day creatine or a no-placebo control. After a 7-day wash-in, the creatine group gained 0.51 ± 1.79 kg more lean body mass. After that, both groups gained 2 kg during 12 weeks of training, with no between-group growth difference. Grade B.

What the larger review found

Delpino and colleagues pooled 35 randomized studies with 1,192 participants. Overall lean body mass increased by 0.68 kg. With resistance training, the difference was 1.10 kg; without exercise it was 0.03 kg and not statistically significant. Grade A for these pooled outcomes.

The review reported +1.46 kg in males and +0.29 kg in females; the female estimate was not statistically significant. Results should not be assumed identical across populations.

Bottom line

Timing and measurement explain why scale weight and lean-mass estimates need separate interpretation. Early weight can include water; later training studies can show lean-mass differences. Neither proves an early scale increase is fat or that every short-term DXA increase is new muscle.
