Dandelion root, leaf and tea: benefits, side effects and evidence

Short answer. Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) has a long history of use for digestion and as a mild "water pill", and European regulators accept it as a traditional remedy for those uses. Clinical proof is thin: the only human trial of dandelion taken by mouth on its own is a one-day pilot in 17 people.

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) puts it plainly: "There's no compelling scientific evidence supporting the use of dandelion for any health condition." As a food, dandelion is considered safe for most people. As a concentrated supplement, less is known, and some people should avoid it.

Evidence posture

Traditional-use support exists for urinary and gastrointestinal indications, but direct human efficacy evidence is sparse and plant-part specific.

What dandelion is used for

Dandelion leaves are eaten as salad greens, and the root is dried for tea or roasted as a coffee substitute. NCCIH notes that oral dandelion products are promoted for indigestion, and that oral and topical products are promoted for joint pain.

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) publishes monographs that set out the accepted traditional uses in the EU. It describes these uses as "exclusively based upon long-standing use", meaning tradition, not clinical trials:

  • Dandelion root: relief of mild digestive complaints such as abdominal fullness, flatulence and slow digestion; temporary loss of appetite; and increasing the amount of urine to flush the urinary tract in minor urinary complaints.
  • Dandelion leaf: increasing the amount of urine to flush the urinary tract in minor urinary complaints.

What the evidence supports

Diuretic effect: one small pilot study

In 2009, researchers gave 17 volunteers a fresh dandelion leaf extract (8 mL, three times in a single day) and compared their urination with their own baseline from the two days before. Urination was more frequent in the five hours after the first dose, and the ratio of urine passed to fluid drunk rose after the second dose. The third dose changed nothing.

The authors called the result promising and said further studies were needed. The study had no placebo group, lasted one day, and tested a leaf extract, so it says little about dandelion root, tea, or longer use.

Everything else: no human trials found

We searched PubMed on November 10, 2026 for clinical trials of dandelion in people. Nine records came back. Eight tested herbal mixtures or dandelion applied to the skin, where any effect cannot be credited to dandelion alone. The 2009 pilot was the only trial of dandelion by mouth on its own.

NCCIH notes that preliminary laboratory studies have looked at dandelion extracts for anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and antiviral properties. Laboratory findings do not show that a tea or capsule has the same effect in people.

Root, leaf and tea are not interchangeable

Much of what is written about "dandelion benefits" mixes up plant parts. The one human trial used leaf extract. Most supplements and roasted "dandelion coffee" use the root. Dandelion tea may be made from leaf, root, or both.

LactMed notes that dandelion root contains high concentrations of inulin, a type of fiber, and that the herb contains vitamins and minerals.

Studied context

The human diuretic pilot used a fresh leaf hydroethanolic extract over one day. No human trial of dandelion root, dandelion tea or roasted-root "coffee" turned up in our search, so the effects and safe amounts of those forms have not been tested in a clinical study.

Side effects and safety

As food. NCCIH describes dandelion in the amounts commonly found in food as likely safe. LactMed notes that the US Food and Drug Administration lists dandelion as "generally recognized as safe" as a food.

Reported side effects. LactMed says dandelion can rarely cause allergic reactions, diarrhea and gastrointestinal upset. The EMA monographs list allergic reactions as a possible side effect, with unknown frequency.

Larger amounts. NCCIH says less is known about the safety of taking dandelion in amounts greater than those in food. The EMA notes that tests for reproductive toxicity and carcinogenicity have not been performed.

Who should avoid dandelion or ask a clinician first

  • People allergic to the daisy family (Asteraceae), which includes ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds and daisies. The EMA lists this as a contraindication. NCCIH says the evidence on cross-reactions is conflicting, and that dandelion on the skin may cause reactions in people with eczema.
  • People with gallstones, bile duct obstruction or liver disease. The EMA advises against use because dandelion may stimulate bile secretion.
  • People with kidney failure, diabetes or heart failure. The EMA leaf monograph says use should be avoided because of possible risks from high blood potassium.
  • People told to limit fluids. The traditional urinary use depends on drinking plenty of fluid.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding and children under 12. The EMA does not recommend use because safety data are lacking. NCCIH says little is known about use in amounts above those in food during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Drug interactions

The EMA monographs list no reported interactions. NCCIH says there are theoretical reasons to suspect that dandelion might interact with diabetes medicines, anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs and water pills, among others. If you take any of these, talk to your prescriber or pharmacist before using dandelion supplements.

When to see a clinician

The EMA monographs advise consulting a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks, or if fever, painful urination, spasms or blood in the urine appear. Urinary symptoms can signal an infection that a herbal tea will not treat.

Common questions

What is dandelion root good for?

Traditionally it is used for mild digestive complaints, temporary loss of appetite and increasing urine flow. Those uses are accepted in the EU on the basis of long-standing use. We found no human trial testing dandelion root for any of them.

Is dandelion a diuretic?

Possibly, in the short term. One pilot study in 17 people found more frequent urination after a leaf extract over a single day. It had no placebo group, and no longer or larger trial has confirmed it.

Does dandelion tea have side effects?

It can rarely cause allergic reactions, diarrhea and stomach upset. People with daisy-family allergies, gallstones, liver or bile duct disease, kidney failure, diabetes or heart failure should avoid it or ask a clinician first.

Does dandelion tea help with weight loss?

We found no human trial showing that it does. Passing more urine for a few hours changes water balance, not body fat.

Does dandelion detox the liver?

No human trial supports that claim. The EMA advises people with liver disease not to use dandelion root.

Is dandelion tea safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding?

Safety has not been established. The EMA does not recommend dandelion preparations in pregnancy or lactation for lack of data. LactMed considers use during breastfeeding unlikely to harm the infant, while noting there are no data on dandelion's active compounds in breast milk.

Product directness

V-ITAREN lists dandelion root. The direct human diuretic pilot reviewed here studied leaf extract, not root, and V-ITAREN does not disclose dose or extraction details. Product-level efficacy is therefore unverified.
