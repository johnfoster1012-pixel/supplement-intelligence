Caffeine

Evidence reviewed: 2026-10-02

Evidence posture

Good evidence for some endurance and high-intensity intermittent performance outcomes; effects vary by dose, habitual use, timing, and individual response.

What the evidence supports

NIH ODS notes that caffeine can reduce perceived fatigue and help sustain exercise intensity, with the clearest support for endurance and some intermittent high-intensity activities. It is not a universal strength or power enhancer.

Studied context

Sports-performance guidance commonly discusses about 2–6 mg/kg taken before exercise. Lower amounts can work in some people, and higher doses generally increase adverse effects without reliably improving performance further.

Safety context

Caffeine can cause insomnia, anxiety, jitteriness, gastrointestinal symptoms, and increased heart rate. Total intake from all sources matters, especially in products containing coffee, guarana, green tea, or yerba mate.

Common questions

Does caffeine improve exercise performance?

Yes, for many kinds of exercise. A 2019 umbrella review of 21 meta-analyses found caffeine improved endurance, muscle strength, muscle endurance and power, with moderate-quality evidence for those outcomes. Most of the studies were in young men (grade A).

Does a small amount of caffeine still help endurance?

It appears to. A 2026 analysis of 48 randomized trials found that both lower and moderate amounts shortened endurance time-trial times compared with placebo, with moderate amounts giving a more consistent effect. No eligible trials tested high amounts on these outcomes (grade A).

What side effects can caffeine cause?

Caffeine can cause trouble sleeping, anxiety, jitteriness, stomach upset and a faster heart rate, as the safety section above notes. Total intake from all sources matters, including coffee, guarana, green tea and yerba mate in combination products.

Product directness

Several current products contain coffee or other caffeine sources, but manufacturer pages generally do not publish total caffeine content. Trial-equivalent performance or safety claims therefore cannot be assigned to those finished products.

Review-level evidence highlights

Wake up and smell the coffee: caffeine supplementation and exercise performance-an umbrella review of 21 published meta-analyses (Umbrella review of meta-analyses; PMID 30926628)

Population/scope: 11 reviews containing 21 meta-analyses of exercise-performance outcomes

Evidence scope: Aerobic endurance, strength, muscular endurance, power, jumping, and exercise speed

Finding: Caffeine showed ergogenic effects across several performance domains, with the strongest and most consistent evidence in endurance-related outcomes.

Limitations: Prediction intervals were not uniformly positive and evidence quality ranged from moderate to low/very low for some outcomes; individual response varies.

Directness: Relevant human evidence · verified 2026-10-02

Important: Ingredient-level evidence is not proof that a finished product has the same effect. Dose, form, population, duration, and formulation must match.
