Magnesium glycinate vs citrate: what studies actually compare

Short answer. No trial has tested magnesium glycinate against magnesium citrate head to head, so neither is proven better. Citrate absorbed better than oxide in small studies. One trial found bisglycinate slightly improved poor sleep versus placebo. Unabsorbed magnesium can loosen stools, but no study shows which of the two is gentler. Not medical advice.

What is the difference between magnesium glycinate and citrate?

The difference is what the magnesium is bound to. Magnesium glycinate, also sold as bisglycinate or diglycinate, is magnesium bound to the amino acid glycine. Magnesium citrate is magnesium bound to citric acid. Labels should state elemental magnesium, the amount of magnesium itself, so compare that number rather than the total compound weight. [NIH ODS; PMID 40918053]

No study we reviewed compared glycinate with bisglycinate, so the names are treated as one family here. [PMID 7815675; PMID 40918053]

Which is absorbed better, magnesium glycinate or citrate?

Nobody has shown a clear winner. Small human studies compared citrate with oxide, diglycinate with oxide, or citrate with an unnamed amino-acid chelate, but not glycinate with citrate. Citrate beat oxide on urine and blood magnesium in two studies. Better absorption on a lab test also does not prove better sleep or bowel results. [PMID 2407766; PMID 14596323; PMID 7815675]

NIH ODS says forms that dissolve well in liquid are generally absorbed more completely, based on small studies of aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride versus oxide or sulfate. It does not mention glycinate. [NIH ODS]

Is magnesium glycinate better for sleep than citrate?

That has not been shown. One trial gave 155 adults with self-reported poor sleep 250 mg of elemental magnesium as bisglycinate or a placebo. After 4 weeks, insomnia scores improved slightly more with magnesium, a small effect size of 0.2. The trial had no citrate group, so it cannot say glycinate beats citrate. [PMID 40918053]

Insomnia Severity Index change at week 4 was −3.9 (95% CI −5.8 to −2.0) with bisglycinate versus −2.3 (−4.1 to −0.4) with placebo, P=0.049. Other psychological outcomes did not differ. [PMID 40918053] Grade B (one trial, small effect).

Is magnesium citrate better for constipation?

No trial we found compared them for constipation. NIH ODS says magnesium salts that are not absorbed can have a laxative effect, and it names carbonate, chloride, gluconate and oxide as the forms most often linked to diarrhea. Neither “glycinate is gentler” nor “citrate works better” has been tested head to head. [NIH ODS]

Grade B for the general laxative effect of unabsorbed magnesium salts. Any glycinate-versus-citrate gut claim is Grade C (no direct comparison).

Do magnesium glycinate or citrate lower blood pressure?

Magnesium supplements as a group lowered blood pressure a little on average. A review of 38 randomized trials with 2,709 people found −2.81 mm Hg systolic and −2.05 mm Hg diastolic. Results varied a lot, there was no effect in people with normal blood pressure, and the review did not show that glycinate or citrate works better. [PMID 41000008]

95% confidence intervals: systolic −4.32 to −1.29, diastolic −3.23 to −0.88. No dose-response was found. [PMID 41000008] The FDA allows this claim only with the caveat that “the evidence is inconsistent and inconclusive.” [NIH ODS] Grade B: blood pressure is a surrogate outcome and the results were mixed.

Which form causes fewer side effects?

There is no head-to-head answer. In 12 patients after bowel surgery, all tolerated diglycinate better than oxide, but citrate was not tested. NIH ODS says high supplement amounts of magnesium can cause diarrhea, nausea and cramping, and very high amounts can be dangerous, especially with reduced kidney function. [PMID 7815675; NIH ODS]

Signs of magnesium toxicity listed by NIH ODS include low blood pressure, vomiting, trouble breathing, irregular heartbeat and cardiac arrest, with higher risk in people with impaired kidney function. [NIH ODS]

What amounts did studies use, and what is the upper limit?

Walker used 300 mg of elemental magnesium a day and Schuster used 250 mg a day as bisglycinate. These are the amounts studied, not personal instructions. NIH ODS sets the adult upper limit for magnesium from supplements and medicines at 350 mg a day. That limit does not count magnesium from food. [PMID 14596323; PMID 40918053; NIH ODS]

How should I read the evidence grades?

  • Grade A: consistent randomized human evidence for that exact outcome.
  • Grade B: human evidence that is limited, mixed, short, small, from a single trial, or surrogate-only (a lab or scan measure instead of how people feel or function).
  • Grade C: animal, cell, mechanistic or anecdotal evidence, comparisons between separate trials, or no human evidence.

We grade the specific claim, not the compound. One Phase 3 trial, or a surrogate outcome alone, is Grade B.
