Magnesium glycinate vs citrate: what studies actually compare

A large direct magnesium glycinate-versus-citrate outcomes trial is missing from the evidence reviewed. Small absorption studies and separate placebo-controlled trials cannot establish which form is better for sleep, constipation, or blood pressure. This page is not medical advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between magnesium glycinate and magnesium citrate?

Glycinate, bisglycinate, and diglycinate are closely related names for magnesium bound to glycine. Citrate is magnesium bound to citric acid. Elemental magnesium is the magnesium amount itself, rather than the full compound weight. [NIH ODS; reviewed studies]

Which form is absorbed better?

A clear glycinate-versus-citrate winner is not established here. Small human studies mainly compared citrate with oxide, diglycinate with oxide, or citrate with an amino-acid chelate not specified as glycinate. Absorption findings do not establish better sleep, bowel habits, or blood pressure. [PMIDs 2407766, 14596323, 7815675]

Is magnesium glycinate better for sleep than citrate?

That is not established. A bisglycinate trial found a small sleep-symptom improvement versus placebo, but did not compare citrate. A large direct glycinate-versus-citrate outcomes trial is missing from the evidence reviewed. [PMID 40918053]

Is magnesium citrate better as a laxative?

Citrate salts can have a laxative effect, but comparative superiority over glycinate is not established here. The evidence reviewed does not include a head-to-head bowel-habit trial of glycinate versus citrate. [NIH ODS]

Do these forms lower blood pressure?

Magnesium supplements as a class showed a modest average blood-pressure reduction in a systematic review, with substantial differences among studies. That result is not specific to glycinate or citrate and does not establish which form is better. [PMID 41000008]

What amounts did studies use, and what is the official supplement limit?

Walker studied 300 mg elemental magnesium daily; Schuster studied 250 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate daily. These are research protocols, not personal instructions. NIH ODS lists an adult upper limit of 350 mg/day from supplements and medications, excluding food. [PMIDs 14596323, 40918053; NIH ODS]

How to read the evidence

  • Grade A: a published Phase 3 randomized trial, or a published systematic review of randomized trials for nutrient outcomes.
  • Grade B: Phase 2 trials, exploratory analyses, small human bioavailability studies, or observational cohorts.
  • Grade C: uncontrolled reports, animal or laboratory inference, or comparisons between unmatched trials.

Grade A for magnesium supplements as a class does not establish a form-specific advantage. Absorption and sleep findings here are Grade B; indirect superiority claims are Grade C.

What the names mean

Magnesium glycinate, bisglycinate, and diglycinate describe magnesium bound to glycine; citrate is bound to citric acid. Labels and studies should identify elemental magnesium, not merely total compound weight. There is no clinical glycinate-versus-bisglycinate head-to-head in the evidence reviewed. [NIH ODS]

What absorption studies actually compared

Citrate versus oxide: Lindberg

Healthy volunteers received an oral 25 mmol load. Citrate was more soluble and produced a higher rise in urinary magnesium than oxide. Grade B for bioavailability; this was not glycinate. [PMID 2407766]

Citrate versus an unspecified amino-acid chelate: Walker

This randomized double-blind study included 46 healthy people and used 300 mg elemental magnesium daily as citrate, oxide, an amino-acid chelate, or placebo for 60 days. Citrate and chelate showed greater urinary absorption than oxide; citrate produced the highest mean serum magnesium, while erythrocyte magnesium did not differ. The chelate was not specified as glycinate. Grade B. [PMID 14596323]

Diglycinate versus oxide after ileal resection

In 12 patients, overall absorption was 23.5% with isotope-labelled chelate versus 22.8% with oxide. Among four patients with greatest oxide impairment it was 23.5% versus 11.8%; all tolerated chelate better. This special-population comparison does not establish superiority to citrate. Grade B. [PMID 7815675]

NIH ODS says forms dissolving well in liquid are generally absorbed more completely, citing small studies of aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride versus oxide or sulfate. The reviewed passage does not rank glycinate above citrate.

Sleep: a modest placebo comparison

Schuster randomized 155 adults with self-reported poor sleep to 250 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate or placebo. At week 4, Insomnia Severity Index change was −3.9 (95% CI −5.8 to −2.0) versus −2.3 (−4.1 to −0.4), P=0.049; Cohen's d=0.2. Other psychological outcomes did not significantly differ. There was no citrate arm. Grade B. [PMID 40918053]

Constipation and tolerability

Citrate salts can have a laxative effect, but the evidence includes no direct glycinate-versus-citrate bowel-habit trial. Marketing that glycinate is gentler is not established by a large comparative trial. [NIH ODS; PMID 7815675]

Blood pressure: class evidence, not a form ranking

A review of 38 randomized trials with 2,709 participants found mean changes of −2.81 mm Hg systolic (95% CI −4.32 to −1.29) and −2.05 diastolic (−3.23 to −0.88). Results varied, normal-blood-pressure groups were not statistically significant, and no dose-response was found. Grade A for magnesium supplements as a class, not either form. [PMID 41000008]

“Consuming diets with adequate magnesium may reduce the risk of high blood pressure (hypertension). However, FDA has concluded that the evidence is inconsistent and inconclusive.”

Safety and remaining gaps

High supplemental amounts often cause diarrhea, nausea, and cramping. Very high amounts can cause low blood pressure, vomiting, breathing difficulty, irregular heartbeat, cardiac arrest, and greater risk with impaired kidney function. A large independent direct clinical comparison remains missing.
