Lemon balm benefits, side effects and evidence

Short answer. Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) is a mint-family herb taken as a tea or extract for stress, sleep and digestion. Its best human evidence is for easing anxiety and low mood: a pooled analysis of randomized trials favored it over placebo, but the trials were few, small and inconsistent, and the authors said the results "should be interpreted with caution."

Evidence for sleep is thinner, a lemon balm cream has some support for cold sores, and the largest trial of memory in older adults found no benefit. European regulators accept lemon balm leaf as a traditional remedy, while stating there is "insufficient evidence from clinical trials." It appears to be well tolerated, though it may reduce alertness and is not recommended in pregnancy.

Evidence posture

Promising but heterogeneous human evidence for anxiety and depressive symptoms; preparation differences and a small trial base limit broad mood or sleep claims.

What lemon balm is used for

Lemon balm leaves have a lemon scent and are used fresh or dried in teas, tinctures, capsules and creams. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) publishes a monograph setting out the traditional uses accepted in the EU for lemon balm leaf, which it calls melissa leaf:

  • relief of mild symptoms of mental stress, and as an aid to sleep;
  • mild digestive complaints, including bloating and flatulence.

The EMA describes these uses as "exclusively based upon long-standing use". Its public summary explains what that means: the effect is plausible and the herb has been used safely for at least 30 years, but clinical trial evidence is insufficient.

What the evidence supports

Anxiety and low mood: the strongest evidence, with caveats

A 2021 systematic review pooled the randomized trials of lemon balm in people with symptoms of anxiety or depression published up to October 2020. Lemon balm improved anxiety scores (standardized mean difference −0.98, 95% confidence interval −1.63 to −0.33) and depression scores (−0.47, −0.73 to −0.21) compared with placebo, without serious side effects. The authors judged it possibly effective, "particularly in the acute setting".

They also listed the limits: a small number of trials, different methods, and a high level of inconsistency between results. The trials used different extracts and doses in different groups of patients. Two examples:

  • In 80 people with chronic stable angina, 3 g a day for 8 weeks lowered depression, anxiety, stress and sleep-disturbance scores compared with placebo.
  • In 60 people with type 2 diabetes and depressive symptoms, 700 mg a day of an extract for 12 weeks improved depression and anxiety scores compared with placebo. Only 44 participants finished the trial.

These are short trials in specific patient groups. They do not show that lemon balm treats an anxiety disorder or depression.

Stress and calmness after a single dose

Three small crossover studies from one UK research group gave single doses to 18 to 20 healthy volunteers. A 600 mg dose of extract increased self-rated calmness during a laboratory stress test. The results were not all favorable: in the first study the highest dose (900 mg) reduced alertness at every time point, and some doses slowed performance on timed memory tasks. A later study using a different dried-leaf preparation found better memory and calmness at its highest dose (1,600 mg), and the authors noted that preparations of the same plant can behave differently.

Sleep: accepted by tradition, lightly tested

Sleep is one of the EMA's accepted traditional uses, yet the trial evidence for lemon balm on its own is limited:

  • A 2024 crossover trial in 30 people found lower insomnia scores after two weeks of a lemon balm extract than after placebo (6.8 against 9.7 on the Insomnia Severity Index). The lead author declared a role at a nutraceutical company.
  • The angina trial above reported less sleep disturbance with lemon balm. The diabetes trial found no significant difference in sleep quality.
  • A 15-day study in 20 volunteers reported less insomnia, but it had no placebo group, and its authors said future studies needed one.
  • Other sleep trials tested lemon balm combined with valerian or other herbs, so the result cannot be credited to lemon balm.

We found no pooled analysis of lemon balm alone for sleep.

Cold sores: a cream, not a tea

In a 1999 placebo-controlled trial, 66 people with recurrent cold sores applied a cream containing 1% dried lemon balm leaf extract four times a day for five days. Symptom scores on day two were lower with the lemon balm cream (4.03 against 4.94). A 2025 systematic review of herbal creams for cold sores found that lemon balm preparations consistently reduced pain and swelling, while noting that the trials were small and most raised some concerns about bias.

This applies to a cream put on the skin. It says nothing about drinking lemon balm tea or taking capsules for cold sores.

Memory and dementia: mostly negative

  • A 2003 trial in 42 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease reported better cognitive scores after four months of lemon balm extract than with placebo.
  • A much larger 2023 trial followed 323 older adults with subjective or mild cognitive impairment for 96 weeks. It found no significant difference in cognitive measures between a lemon balm extract and placebo.
  • For agitation in dementia, a blinded trial of lemon balm oil aromatherapy in 114 care-home residents found it no better than placebo. A 2020 Cochrane review of aromatherapy in dementia, including four lemon balm studies, found no convincing evidence of benefit.

Cholesterol, blood pressure and blood sugar: early and inconsistent

Two meta-analyses have pooled small trials. A 2024 analysis of 5 trials (302 patients) found lower triglycerides, total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol with lemon balm. A 2020 analysis of 7 trials found lower total cholesterol and systolic blood pressure, but no significant change in triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, fasting blood sugar or HbA1c. The 2020 authors described a "serious risk of bias" in the underlying trials. The two analyses disagree on triglycerides and LDL, and neither supports using lemon balm in place of prescribed treatment.

Tea, capsules, cream and oil are not interchangeable

"Lemon balm" covers several different products, and the research on one does not transfer to another.

Studied context

Clinical studies use varied Melissa officinalis preparations, doses, durations and populations. Many trials tested leaf extracts for 2 to 12 weeks in specific patient groups; the single-dose studies were done in healthy volunteers. The evidence does not establish that leaf tea, standardized extracts, extracts from other plant parts and combination products are interchangeable.

Side effects and safety

As food. LactMed, the US government's lactation database, notes that the Food and Drug Administration lists lemon balm as "generally recognized as safe" as a food flavoring.

Reported side effects. The EMA monograph lists no known side effects for traditional leaf preparations. LactMed describes lemon balm as generally well tolerated in adults, with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dizziness and wheezing reported occasionally. The pooled anxiety and depression trials reported no serious side effects.

Alertness and driving. The EMA says lemon balm may impair the ability to drive and use machines, and that affected people should not do either. This fits the single-dose study in which the highest dose reduced alertness.

Liver. LiverTox, the US National Institutes of Health database on liver injury, says lemon balm has not been linked to raised liver enzymes or to clinically apparent liver injury.

Longer use. In the 96-week trial of one extract, vital signs and physical and neurological measures did not differ from placebo. That covers one preparation. The EMA notes that tests for reproductive toxicity and carcinogenicity have not been performed.

Isolated case reports. One report describes restlessness, tremor and sweating in a 30-year-old after stopping long-term lemon balm use. Another describes a temporary heart rhythm problem in a 25-year-old taking a seven-ingredient supplement that included lemon balm and valerian; it resolved after she stopped. Single cases cannot show cause, and the second cannot be pinned on lemon balm.

Who should avoid lemon balm or ask a clinician first

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. The EMA does not recommend use because safety has not been established. LactMed says there are no data on lemon balm's components in breast milk or on its safety when used alone by nursing mothers, while judging the small amounts expected in milk unlikely to be harmful at usual doses.
  • Children under 12. The EMA says use has not been established for lack of data.
  • People with thyroid disease or taking thyroid medicine. The EMA notes that laboratory and animal studies indicate a water extract of lemon balm leaf may inhibit thyroid-stimulating hormone, and that the relevance to people is not known. No clinical trial has tested this, so it is worth raising with your clinician.
  • Anyone who needs to stay alert. See the note on alertness and driving above.
  • People allergic to lemon balm. The EMA lists hypersensitivity as a contraindication.

Drug interactions

The EMA monograph says no interaction data are available. That means interactions have not been studied, not that none exist. Because lemon balm may reduce alertness, check with a pharmacist before combining it with sleep medicines, sedatives or other products that make you drowsy.

When to see a clinician

The EMA advises consulting a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks or get worse while using lemon balm. Ongoing anxiety, low mood or insomnia can have causes that a herbal tea will not treat, and palpitations should always be checked.

Common questions

What is lemon balm good for?

Traditionally, mild stress, sleep and mild digestive complaints such as bloating. The best trial evidence is for easing anxiety and low mood over a few weeks, and it comes from a small number of small trials with inconsistent results.

Does lemon balm help you sleep?

Possibly. European regulators accept it as a traditional sleep aid, and one 30-person trial found lower insomnia scores than with placebo. Other trials were mixed, lacked a placebo group, or tested lemon balm together with other herbs.

Does lemon balm help with anxiety?

Pooled trials found lower anxiety scores than with placebo, particularly over short periods. The authors urged caution because the trials were few and varied widely. It has not been shown to treat an anxiety disorder.

What are the side effects of lemon balm tea?

It is generally well tolerated. Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dizziness and wheezing have been reported occasionally, and it may reduce alertness. It is not recommended during pregnancy, while breastfeeding, or for children under 12.

Does lemon balm help with weight loss?

We found no human trial testing lemon balm for weight loss. In a 12-week trial in people with type 2 diabetes, body measurements did not differ significantly from placebo. For how a supplement's trial results compare with a prescription weight-loss drug, see berberine vs Ozempic.

Does lemon balm affect the thyroid?

In laboratory and animal studies a water extract inhibited thyroid-stimulating hormone. Whether this happens in people is not known, and we found no clinical trial of lemon balm for any thyroid condition. People with thyroid disease should ask their clinician before using it regularly.

Can you drink lemon balm tea every day?

The EMA monograph describes lemon balm tea one to three times a day for adults, and advises seeing a doctor if symptoms last longer than two weeks. Most of the trials reviewed here lasted 2 to 12 weeks, so they say little about long-term daily use.

Product directness

V-CONTROL lists 'lemon balm fruit extract' without dose or standardization. The reviewed EMA monograph centers on Melissa leaf and the clinical literature uses varied preparations, so direct applicability to the finished product is not established.
