Uva ursi (bearberry) benefits, side effects and UTI evidence

Short answer. Uva ursi (Arctostaphylos uva-ursi, also called bearberry) is a leaf taken as a tea or extract, mostly for urinary tract infections (UTIs). It has been tested in two large randomized trials in women with suspected UTIs, and neither supports using it in place of standard care. In one, it did no better than placebo for symptoms. In the other, women who started with uva ursi instead of an antibiotic used fewer antibiotics but had a higher symptom burden, and more of them developed a kidney infection (8 against 2, a difference that fell short of statistical significance).

European regulators accept bearberry leaf only as a traditional remedy for mild, recurring bladder symptoms in adult women, for no more than a week, after a doctor has ruled out anything serious. We found no human trial of uva ursi for water retention, weight loss or kidney stones. It is not recommended in pregnancy, while breastfeeding, for men, for anyone under 18, or for people with kidney disorders.

Evidence posture

Human trials do not establish uva ursi as an equivalent treatment for uncomplicated UTI; symptom benefit was absent or inferior to antibiotic therapy in reviewed trials.

What uva ursi is used for

Uva ursi is a low evergreen shrub. The part used is the dried leaf, which contains a compound called arbutin; European pharmacopoeia-grade leaf contains at least 7% arbutin. In the body, arbutin is converted to forms of hydroquinone that are passed in the urine, and hydroquinone is thought to be what acts against bacteria.

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) recognizes one traditional use for bearberry leaf: relief of the symptoms of mild, recurring lower urinary tract infections in women, such as burning when passing urine or passing urine often, "after serious conditions have been excluded by a medical doctor." The EMA is explicit that this rests on "long-standing use," and its assessment describes the scientific evidence for efficacy and safety in humans as "very poor."

Uva ursi also appears in "water pill," detox and kidney-cleanse supplements. A 2014 review of 184 websites selling supplements for kidney disease found uva ursi among the ten most common plant ingredients, and concluded those ingredients "were not adequately studied in humans."

A naming trap. Several trials of "Vaccinium arctostaphylos" (Caucasian whortleberry) for blood sugar, cholesterol and blood pressure turn up in searches for uva ursi. That is a different plant. Those results say nothing about uva ursi.

What the evidence supports

Treating a UTI: two large trials, neither favorable

Against placebo (ATAFUTI, 2019). In a UK trial, 382 women aged 18 to 70 with a suspected lower UTI took uva ursi extract or placebo, with or without advice to take ibuprofen. All were given a back-up antibiotic prescription to use if needed. The planned dose was 3,600 mg of extract a day, providing 686 mg of arbutin, for 3 to 5 days. Uva ursi made no difference to how severe urinary frequency symptoms were on days 2 to 4 (difference −0.06 on the symptom score, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.21). Antibiotic use was 39.9% with uva ursi and 47.4% with placebo, a difference that was not statistically significant. No safety concerns and no kidney infections were recorded.

Against an antibiotic (REGATTA, 2021). In a German trial in 42 family practices, 398 women with a suspected uncomplicated UTI were given either uva ursi tablets for 5 days (two tablets three times a day) or a single dose of the antibiotic fosfomycin. Women starting on uva ursi took 63.6% fewer antibiotic courses over 28 days. But their total symptom burden in the first week was 136.5% of that in the antibiotic group, which failed the trial's pre-set test for being no worse than the antibiotic. Eight women in the uva ursi group developed pyelonephritis (a kidney infection) against two in the fosfomycin group. That difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.067), but the authors concluded uva ursi "led to a higher symptom burden and more safety concerns than fosfomycin."

Taken together: uva ursi did not relieve UTI symptoms better than a dummy capsule, and using it first instead of an antibiotic meant more days of symptoms.

Preventing repeat UTIs: one old trial of a mixture

Germany's health technology assessment agency, IQWiG, reviewed herbal remedies for recurring bladder infections in 2022. It found only isolated studies of uva ursi, each in combination with another herb. It reported "a hint of benefit" compared with placebo for one preparation of uva ursi leaf with dandelion, and noted that this product was either impossible to find, inadequately described or no longer on the market. A result for a mixture cannot be credited to uva ursi alone, and we found no prevention trial of uva ursi by itself.

How it is supposed to work

Small studies in healthy volunteers confirm that arbutin from bearberry leaf reaches the urine. In one study of 16 volunteers, about 65% of the arbutin dose was recovered in urine as hydroquinone and related compounds; in a three-person study, more than 75% appeared within 24 hours, mainly in bound (conjugated) forms.

Older laboratory work found free hydroquinone in urine only when the urine was alkaline, which is why some sources advise taking uva ursi with baking soda. A later four-volunteer study suggested bacteria can release hydroquinone themselves and that making the urine alkaline "seems not to be a prerequisite." The EMA monograph gives no instruction to alkalinize urine. None of these studies measured whether infections got better, and the two large trials above are the better guide to that.

Water retention, bloating and weight loss: no human trials

Uva ursi is often described as a natural diuretic. We found no human trial that measured urine output, water retention, bloating or body weight with uva ursi. The animal evidence is mixed: one rat study found that a water extract increased urine flow, while another, described in the EMA assessment, found a leaf infusion had no effect on urine volume.

Any weight lost through a diuretic is water, not fat, and it returns with normal drinking. Our pages on dandelion, horsetail and hibiscus cover other herbs sold for the same purpose.

Kidney stones and kidney "cleansing": no human trials

We found no human trial of uva ursi for kidney stones. A 1994 rat study of seven herbs promoted for stones, including uva ursi, concluded that "more effective and equally innocuous substances are well known." The EMA lists kidney disorders as a reason not to take bearberry leaf, which sits badly with its use in kidney-cleanse products.

Tea, capsules and extracts

How much arbutin a product delivers depends on the form, and many supplements do not state it.

FormWhat is known
Leaf teaThe EMA's traditional amount for adult women is 1.5 to 4 g of cut leaf in 150 ml of water, 2 to 4 times a day, with a maximum of 8 g a day. It suggests an infusion or a cold-water soak rather than boiling the leaves, to draw out fewer tannins, which are blamed for stomach upset.
Powdered leafThe EMA's traditional amount is 700 to 1,050 mg twice a day, with a maximum of 1.75 g a day.
Standardized dry extractThe form used in both large trials. The EMA's traditional range corresponds to 200 to 840 mg of hydroquinone derivatives (counted as arbutin) a day. The UK trial planned 686 mg of arbutin a day.
Blends that list "uva ursi leaf"Usually no arbutin content is given, so the dose cannot be compared with any trial.

Whatever the form, the EMA says bearberry leaf is "not to be used for more than one week," and that a doctor should be consulted if symptoms last more than four days. These are the regulator's traditional-use amounts, not a recommendation from us.

Studied context

The clinical trials involved adult women with suspected uncomplicated urinary tract infection and used defined uva ursi extracts with a known arbutin content for 3 to 5 days. The results concern strategies for treating a symptomatic UTI. They do not cover men, children, pregnancy, long-term use, tea, or general "urinary wellness" and diuretic claims.

Side effects and safety

Stomach upset. The EMA monograph lists nausea, vomiting and stomach ache, with frequency unknown. Its assessment attributes these to the leaf's high tannin content, which it puts at 10 to 20%.

Urine color. The EMA notes that bearberry leaf "may cause a greenish-brown coloration of the urine."

In the trials. Over 3 to 5 days of use, the UK trial reported no safety concerns, and the German trial reported similar rates of adverse events in both groups, apart from the kidney infections described above.

Liver. LiverTox, the US National Institutes of Health database on liver injury, says uva ursi "has not been specifically linked" to raised liver enzymes or to clinically apparent liver injury. The EMA assessment repeats warnings from older reference books that prolonged use or overdose could harm the liver; for this it cites reference books, pointing to the tannin content and to hydroquinone, rather than case reports.

Hydroquinone. Concern about hydroquinone is one reason the EMA assessment says bearberry leaf "should not be used for prolonged periods." A 2013 risk assessment estimated that the free hydroquinone produced by a standard daily dose stays well below the level its authors considered a negligible risk, and judged use at recommended doses safe; those authors worked for a German herbal medicine manufacturer. The EMA monograph states that adequate tests of genotoxicity have not been done and that carcinogenicity has not been tested.

Long-term use. One case report describes a 56-year-old woman who developed damage to the central retina in both eyes (bull's-eye maculopathy) after taking uva ursi for three years. A single case cannot prove cause, but it is the kind of use the one-week limit is meant to prevent.

Detox regimens. Another case report describes a 67-year-old man admitted to intensive care with a dangerously low blood sodium level after a five-day "kidney detox" of more than a gallon of fluid a day plus a tea containing uva ursi, juniper and other herbs. The report cannot separate the effect of the herbs from the very large amount of fluid.

Who should avoid uva ursi or ask a clinician first

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women. The EMA does not recommend use, because safety has not been established. LactMed, the NIH database on drugs and breastfeeding, says uva ursi "should generally be avoided during breastfeeding because of a lack of data and its potential toxicity."
  • Children and teenagers under 18. Not recommended by the EMA.
  • Men. Not recommended by the EMA, because urinary symptoms in men need medical assessment.
  • People with kidney disorders. A listed contraindication in the EMA monograph.
  • Anyone with fever, back or side pain, blood in the urine, or symptoms that are getting worse. These can signal a kidney infection and need prompt medical care, not a herbal remedy.
  • Anyone who has reacted to bearberry before.

Drug interactions

The EMA monograph says none have been reported. That reflects a lack of documented cases, not studies showing it is safe to combine. The EMA assessment mentions a suggestion in one reference book that uva ursi could blunt the effect of some diuretic medicines, but did not include it in the monograph because no case report supports it. A 2026 laboratory study in cultured bladder cancer cells found that hydroquinone and the UTI antibiotic nitrofurantoin together caused more cell damage than either alone; this has not been tested in people. Tell your prescriber or pharmacist if you take uva ursi alongside any medicine.

When to see a clinician

See a clinician for any suspected UTI if you are pregnant, male, under 18, have diabetes or kidney disease, or have had repeated infections. Seek care promptly for fever, chills, pain in the back or side, nausea or vomiting, or blood in the urine, and if bladder symptoms have not improved within a few days.

Common questions

Does uva ursi cure a UTI?

The trials do not show that. In a 382-woman trial it was no better than placebo for symptoms. In a 398-woman trial, women who took it first instead of an antibiotic had a higher symptom burden, and eight developed a kidney infection against two on the antibiotic.

How long can you take uva ursi?

The EMA says no more than one week, and that a doctor should be consulted if symptoms last more than four days. Longer use has not been tested in trials, and one case of eye damage was reported after three years of use.

Is uva ursi a diuretic?

It is sold as one, but we found no human trial measuring urine output or water retention. Two rat studies gave opposite results.

Does uva ursi help with weight loss?

We found no human trial of uva ursi for weight loss. Any weight lost through a diuretic effect would be water, not fat. For what trials show about weight-loss supplements and prescription drugs, see Berberine vs Ozempic.

What are the side effects of uva ursi tea?

Nausea, vomiting and stomach ache are the ones the EMA lists, linked to the tannins in the leaf. It can also turn urine greenish-brown.

Do you need baking soda with uva ursi?

Older laboratory studies found it active only in alkaline urine. A small later study suggested this may not be necessary, and the EMA monograph does not call for it. Neither approach has been shown to clear infections in a trial.

Is uva ursi safe in pregnancy?

It is not recommended. The EMA says safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established, and a UTI in pregnancy always needs medical care.

Is bearberry the same as uva ursi?

Yes. Both names refer to Arctostaphylos uva-ursi, and the leaf is the part used. It is a different plant from cranberry, and from Vaccinium arctostaphylos.
