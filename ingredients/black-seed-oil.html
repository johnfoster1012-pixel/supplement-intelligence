Black seed oil benefits, side effects and evidence

Short answer. Black seed (Nigella sativa, also called black cumin) is one of the better-studied herbal supplements. Pooled analyses of dozens of randomized trials found that it modestly lowers blood sugar, cholesterol and blood pressure, and reduces body weight by about 1.8 kg compared with placebo.

The effects are small, the trials are mostly short and vary widely in quality and results, and it is not a replacement for prescribed treatment. It is generally well tolerated. People on diabetes, blood pressure or blood-thinning medicines should check with a clinician first.

Evidence posture

Multiple meta-analyses of randomized trials show modest improvements in blood sugar, lipids, blood pressure and body weight; effects are small, trials are short, and results vary widely between studies.

What black seed oil is

Black seed comes from Nigella sativa, a flowering plant native to Eastern Europe and the Middle East. The seeds are used as a spice and have a long history in traditional medicine. Supplements come as cold-pressed oil, oil capsules, or ground seed powder. One of the oil's known constituents is thymoquinone.

Despite the name black cumin, it is a different plant from ordinary cumin.

What the evidence supports

Unlike most herbs on this site, black seed has been tested in enough randomized trials for several pooled analyses. The figures below compare black seed with placebo or control.

Blood sugar

A 2024 analysis pooled 30 trials and found that black seed lowered fasting blood sugar and HbA1c, a measure of longer-term blood sugar. It did not change insulin levels or a standard measure of insulin resistance. The results varied a great deal from one trial to the next, and the authors asked for longer and more robust studies to settle the best dose and duration.

Cholesterol and triglycerides

A 2024 analysis of 34 trials with 2,278 participants found lower total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, and higher HDL cholesterol. The authors suggested it "may be suggested as an adjuvant" to treatment, meaning an addition and not a replacement.

Blood pressure

Across 11 trials lasting about 8 weeks on average, black seed lowered systolic pressure by about 3.3 mmHg and diastolic pressure by about 2.8 mmHg more than control. The effect was larger with seed powder than with oil. The authors said more evidence was needed, particularly on long-term effects.

Body weight

A 2018 analysis of 10 trials found about 1.76 kg more weight loss than placebo and a small reduction in BMI, with no significant change in waist size. Results again varied widely between trials. That is in the same range as other supplements with weight data and far below prescription weight-loss drugs; see Do GLP-1 supplements work?

Fatty liver and inflammation

In five trials with 358 people with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, black seed improved liver enzymes and the grade of fatty liver, but not cholesterol or triglycerides; the authors called for larger, high-quality trials. A separate analysis of 20 trials found lower blood markers of inflammation such as C-reactive protein. Lower markers are not the same as fewer illnesses, which these trials did not measure.

Why the numbers need caution

  • Trials disagree with each other. In the blood sugar analysis, the statistical measure of disagreement between trials was above 90%, which means the pooled figure hides very different individual results.
  • Trials are short. The blood pressure trials averaged about 8 weeks, and the authors of both the blood pressure and blood sugar analyses called for longer studies.
  • Products differ. Trials used oil, powder and extracts at different doses, so a result for one form may not apply to another.
  • They measured lab values, not whether people stayed healthier.

Studied context

Trials mostly enrolled adults with type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, high cholesterol, raised blood pressure or fatty liver, and gave black seed oil, powder or extract; the blood pressure trials averaged about 8 weeks. Results may not apply to healthy people, to other forms or doses, or to long-term use.

Side effects and safety

Generally well tolerated. LiverTox, the US National Institutes of Health database on liver injury, says black cumin "is well tolerated, is generally recognized as safe and has not been associated with serum enzyme elevations during therapy nor implicated in cases of clinically apparent liver injury."

Skin reactions. LactMed, the NIH database on breastfeeding, notes that black seed oil "can cause allergic contact dermatitis." Several case reports describe skin reactions to the oil, including from cosmetics that contain it.

Rare case reports. One 2024 report describes muscle breakdown with kidney and liver injury after a person consumed black seed oil. A 2026 report describes extensive bruising under the skin in a patient taking black seed oil together with two antiplatelet drugs and chemotherapy; the authors rated the link as "possible," and the patient later restarted a lower dose without further bleeding. Single cases cannot prove cause.

Who should be careful

  • People on diabetes medicines. Black seed lowers blood sugar, so the combination could push it too low.
  • People on blood pressure medicines, for the same reason.
  • People on blood thinners or antiplatelet drugs, or facing surgery, given the bleeding case report.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women. LactMed found no data on black seed in breast milk or in infants. Amounts used in food are a different matter from supplement doses.

Do not stop or reduce a prescribed medicine because you have started black seed oil.

Common questions

What is black seed oil good for?

Pooled trials show modest improvements in blood sugar, cholesterol, blood pressure and body weight. These are small effects, not treatments on their own.

Does black seed oil help with weight loss?

A little. Ten trials pooled together found about 1.8 kg more weight loss than placebo.

What are the side effects of black seed oil?

It is generally well tolerated. Allergic skin reactions are the best-documented problem, and rare case reports describe kidney, muscle and bleeding problems.

Can I take black seed oil with metformin or blood pressure tablets?

Ask your prescriber first. Black seed lowers blood sugar and blood pressure itself, so the combined effect may be stronger than intended.

Is black seed oil or powder better?

For blood pressure, a pooled analysis found a larger effect with seed powder than with oil. For other outcomes the two have not been clearly compared.

How long does black seed oil take to work?

The blood pressure trials averaged about 8 weeks. Longer-term effects have not been established.

Is black seed the same as black cumin?

Yes. Black seed and black cumin are both names for Nigella sativa. It is a different plant from ordinary cumin.

Product directness

No product in this site's catalog lists black seed oil yet. Trials used different oils, powders and extracts at different doses, so a result for one preparation cannot be assumed for another product.
