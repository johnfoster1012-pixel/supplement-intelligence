Fulvic acid benefits, side effects and evidence

Short answer. Fulvic acid is a mix of natural compounds from soil, peat and ancient plant deposits, sold as "fulvic minerals" in drops and powders. The health claims made for it run far ahead of the research. We found no randomized trial of a fulvic acid supplement for energy, mineral absorption, detox, gut health or weight loss.

The human studies that exist are small: a one-week safety study and a skin-cream trial of one laboratory-made form, plus a handful of trials of shilajit, a resin that contains fulvic acid along with many other things. Products made from natural deposits vary in composition and can contain heavy metals, so quality testing matters more here than for most supplements.

Evidence posture

No randomized trials support the main health claims for fulvic acid supplements; human data are limited to short studies of one laboratory-made form and to trials of shilajit, a mixture.

What fulvic acid is

When plants decay over long periods, soil microbes turn them into dark organic material called humic substances. Fulvic acid is the smaller, water-soluble part of that material. It is not a single molecule, and its makeup differs from one source to the next.

Supplements get it from several places:

  • Soil, peat or coal-like deposits, the usual source of "fulvic minerals" drops and powders;
  • Shilajit, a tar-like resin from mountain rock used in Ayurvedic medicine, which contains fulvic acid plus minerals and other compounds;
  • A laboratory-made form called carbohydrate-derived fulvic acid, made from sugar to avoid contaminants from the ground.

This matters because a study of one source does not tell you about another.

What the evidence supports

A 2018 scientific review of fulvic acid described the available research as "minimal" and noted that products like it rarely come "with verified health benefits or dosing schedules established through modern scientific research." Our own PubMed searches for human trials of fulvic acid turned up only the two studies below.

The two human studies of fulvic acid itself

Both tested the laboratory-made form, not a soil-derived mineral product.

  • Safety study (2012). Thirty men with allergies drank increasing amounts, up to 40 mL of a 3.8% solution twice a day for a week. No severe adverse events occurred and blood tests stayed normal. Skin-prick allergy reactions were smaller afterward, which the authors read as an anti-inflammatory effect.
  • Eczema cream trial (2011). Thirty-six people applied a fulvic acid cream or placebo for four weeks. The fulvic acid group improved on the investigators' overall rating, with a short-lived burning sensation as the main side effect. The authors called it a "small exploratory study."

Shilajit trials

Shilajit has more human trials, each small and each of a purified, branded extract:

  • In men aged 45 to 55, 250 mg twice a day for 90 days raised testosterone compared with placebo.
  • In 35 young men, 8 weeks of shilajit raised a blood marker of collagen production.
  • In 60 postmenopausal women with low bone density, 48 weeks of shilajit extract slowed bone loss compared with placebo.

These results cannot be credited to fulvic acid alone, because shilajit is a mixture, and they do not carry over to fulvic mineral drops.

Studied context

Human studies of fulvic acid itself used a laboratory-made form for one week by mouth or four weeks on the skin. Shilajit trials used purified extracts at 250 to 1,000 mg a day for 8 to 48 weeks. No trial has tested soil-derived fulvic mineral drops or powders, and no study of fulvic acid alone has lasted longer than a week by mouth.

Side effects and safety

Short-term. In the one-week safety study, no severe adverse events occurred. The cream caused brief burning in some users.

Heavy metals and variable contents. This is the main concern. A 2026 toxicology paper states that fulvic acid materials from the environment "vary substantially in composition and may contain impurities such as heavy metals." A 2022 laboratory analysis of shilajit sold from India and Pakistan found lead, arsenic and mercury above permitted limits. A 2024 review found most reported shilajit samples within limits, with exceptions, and warned that using it without knowing its metal levels "is not safe."

Genetic safety tests. The laboratory-made form passed standard genotoxicity tests in a 2026 study, apart from a weak effect in one cell test that the authors attributed to the acidity of the solution. The authors were employees of a company with a commercial interest in it. Soil-derived products have not been tested the same way, as far as we found.

Long-term use. Not studied. In parts of China, fulvic acid in drinking water is one of several suspected contributors to Kashin-Beck disease, a bone and joint disorder; the idea rests on animal experiments and its relevance to supplements is unknown.

Who should be careful

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women and children. No safety data exist, and heavy metals are a particular risk for these groups.
  • Anyone taking prescription medicines. Interactions have not been studied. Ask a pharmacist or prescriber.

Choosing a product

If you use fulvic acid, look for a product whose maker publishes a recent third-party certificate of analysis showing lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury results for the batch. A list of dozens of trace minerals is not evidence of benefit, and it is a reason to check for the unwanted ones.

Common questions

What is fulvic acid good for?

No use is established by clinical trials. Early studies of a laboratory-made form suggest an anti-inflammatory effect and possible help with eczema as a cream. The popular claims about energy, absorption and detox have not been tested in people.

What are the side effects of fulvic acid?

A one-week study found no serious side effects. The larger concern is contamination: products from natural deposits can contain heavy metals.

Does fulvic acid remove heavy metals from the body?

We found no human trial showing that. A 2024 review said the data on metal detoxification by these substances "still needs to be clarified," and some products carry metals with them.

Is fulvic acid the same as shilajit?

No. Shilajit is a natural resin that contains fulvic acid along with minerals and other compounds. Shilajit trial results cannot be assumed for fulvic acid drops.

What is the difference between fulvic acid and humic acid?

Both come from the same decayed plant material. Fulvic acid is the smaller part that dissolves in water at any acidity; humic acid is the larger part that does not dissolve in acid.

Does fulvic acid help with weight loss?

We found no human trial of fulvic acid for weight loss. For what trials show about weight-loss supplements, see Do GLP-1 supplements work?

Is it safe to take fulvic acid every day?

Unknown. The longest study of fulvic acid alone by mouth lasted one week.
