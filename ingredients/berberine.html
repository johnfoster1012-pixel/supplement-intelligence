Berberine

Evidence posture

Supportive but heterogeneous evidence for glycemic and lipid outcomes in type 2 diabetes; weight-loss evidence is inconclusive.

What the evidence supports

NCCIH reports some evidence that berberine may improve blood glucose, insulin resistance, and lipid measures in people with type 2 diabetes, but study quality, populations, formulations, and effects vary. Evidence for weight loss is not conclusive.

Berberine is also marketed as a GLP-1 supplement. Our review of berberine sold as a GLP-1 supplement, and of other boosters, covers what the trials show for that use.

Studied context

Published studies use varied formulations and doses, often in people with diabetes or other metabolic disease. Results should not be generalized to all users or all products.

Safety context

Common adverse effects are gastrointestinal. Berberine can interact with medicines and should not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding or given to infants.

Common questions

Does berberine work?

For blood sugar in type 2 diabetes, modestly. A 2022 pooled analysis of 37 randomized trials (3,048 people) found HbA1c about 0.6 percentage points lower and fasting glucose about 0.82 mmol/L (about 15 mg/dL) lower than in control groups. The trials varied in dose, product and other treatments, so this is some evidence, still thin (grade B). For weight, pooled trials show about 0 to 2 kg more weight loss than control, depending on the analysis: 0.88 kg across 23 trials in a 2026 analysis and 2.07 kg across 12 trials in a 2020 one (grade B). Berberine is not an approved diabetes treatment, and products vary in dose and purity.

What are the side effects of berberine?

Stomach and bowel upset is the most common: nausea, bloating, constipation or diarrhea, according to the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH). In a 2008 pilot study in adults with type 2 diabetes, 34.5% of patients had short-lived stomach or bowel effects. In the 2022 pooled diabetes trials, berberine, alone or with diabetes tablets, did not raise the overall rate of side effects or of low blood sugar (grade B). It can interact with medicines, and NCCIH says it should not be used during pregnancy or while breastfeeding or given to infants. If you take a prescription medicine, ask your prescriber before adding it.

Is berberine a natural GLP-1 drug?

No. Berberine has never been compared with a GLP-1 drug in a trial, and it does not act on the GLP-1 receptor the way semaglutide does. Their separate trials are far apart. Pooled berberine trials show about 0 to 2 kg more weight loss than control (grade B). In the STEP 1 trial, adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg lost 14.9% of their body weight over 68 weeks, against 2.4% on placebo, about 12.7 kg more (grade A). These are different trials in different people, not a head-to-head comparison. Do not swap a prescribed medicine for berberine without your clinician. More detail is in berberine vs Ozempic.

Grades: A means consistent randomized human evidence for that outcome, B means human evidence that is limited, mixed or small, and C means no direct human evidence. Answers added November 25, 2026, with every number checked against the published abstracts and NCCIH pages that day.

Product directness

S-BALANCE and NOURISH+ list berberine, but current manufacturer pages do not establish a trial-equivalent berberine dose. Product-level glucose or weight-loss claims therefore remain unverified.

Review-level evidence highlights

Glucose-lowering effect of berberine on type 2 diabetes: A systematic review and meta-analysis (Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials; PMID 36467075)

Population/scope: 37 studies; 3,048 people with type 2 diabetes

Evidence scope: Fasting glucose, HbA1c, 2-hour postprandial glucose, and adverse events

Finding: Pooled analyses found reductions in fasting glucose, HbA1c, and 2-hour postprandial glucose; hypoglycemia risk was not significantly increased.

Limitations: Trials varied in baseline glycemia, dose, formulation, and co-therapy. Findings apply to people with type 2 diabetes and do not establish weight-loss efficacy or finished-product effects.

Directness: Relevant human evidence · verified 2026-10-02

Important: Ingredient-level evidence is not proof that a finished product has the same effect. Dose, form, population, duration, and formulation must match.
