Resveratrol

Evidence posture

Human evidence is heterogeneous and low-certainty for many cardiometabolic outcomes; anti-aging or longevity claims are not established.

What the evidence supports

NCCIH summarizes low-certainty evidence suggesting resveratrol may influence fasting glucose or blood pressure in some people with type 2 diabetes, while other outcomes are neutral and more research is needed.

Studied context

Trials often use concentrated resveratrol doses far above typical dietary exposure. Dose, formulation, health status, and outcome strongly affect interpretation.

Safety context

Oral resveratrol appears generally tolerated in available studies, but it may interact with anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs and supplements.

Common questions

Does resveratrol lower blood sugar?

Possibly, a little, in people with type 2 diabetes. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) calls the evidence low certainty. It cites a 2020 Cochrane review of 3 short studies (50 people) that found no effect on HbA1c, and a 2022 analysis of 19 studies (1,151 people) in which high doses lowered fasting blood sugar (grade B).

Does resveratrol lower blood pressure?

Some pooled studies suggest so. The 2022 analyses that NCCIH cites found lower blood pressure with resveratrol in people with type 2 diabetes, but the studies were short and used concentrated doses far above what food provides (grade B).

Is resveratrol safe?

It appears to be safe when taken by mouth, according to NCCIH, and a 2010 review found no marked toxicity in human studies (grade C). NCCIH notes it may interact with other medicines, especially blood thinners and antiplatelet drugs or supplements.

Product directness

COLLAGEN COMPLEX and NOURISH+ list resveratrol but do not publish a trial-matched dose. Anti-aging, metabolic, or cardiovascular outcomes cannot be inferred from ingredient presence.
