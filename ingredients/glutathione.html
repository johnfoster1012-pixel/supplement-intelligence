Oral glutathione

Evidence posture

Bioavailability is formulation- and duration-dependent; biomarker changes are documented, but clinical health-outcome benefits remain uncertain.

What the evidence supports

Human studies have produced different findings. An older single-dose study found negligible systemic availability, while a 6-month randomized trial reported increased glutathione stores, and newer pharmacokinetic research suggests formulation-dependent absorption.

Studied context

Dose, formulation, duration, and outcome matter. Raising a blood or tissue glutathione marker is not itself proof of a clinical benefit.

Safety context

Short-term studies generally report tolerability, but the evidence base for long-term clinical outcomes and formulation-specific safety is limited.

Common questions

Is glutathione absorbed when taken by mouth?

The studies disagree. A 1992 study in 7 volunteers found no rise in blood glutathione after a single large dose. A 2015 randomized trial found blood glutathione stores rose over 6 months of daily use and fell back after stopping, and a small 2026 crossover trial (14 people), with authors from a supplement maker, found higher blood levels from a micellar form than from standard glutathione (grade B).

Does raising glutathione levels improve health?

That hasn't been shown. The trials measured glutathione and related markers in the blood, not health outcomes such as illness or disease. A higher marker isn't proof of a clinical benefit (grade C).

Is glutathione safe?

Short-term studies report it's well tolerated. In the 2026 trial, 30 days of daily use caused no significant changes in liver or kidney markers in 14 healthy adults. Long-term safety and safety of specific formulations are not well studied (grade B).
